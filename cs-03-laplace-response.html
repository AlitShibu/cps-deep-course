The Laplace Transform & System Response

Chapter 2 used the Laplace transform as a black box, promising "d/dt becomes s" without justifying it. This chapter opens that box just enough to use it confidently, then puts it to real use: given a transfer function, you'll be able to read off — often without doing any calculus at all — whether a system responds quickly or slowly, and whether it settles smoothly or oscillates. This is the chapter where transfer functions stop being abstract algebra and start directly predicting real physical behavior.

Section 3.1

What the Laplace transform actually does

L{f(t)} = F(s) = ∫₀^∞ f(t)·e^(−st) dt

Rather than working through this integral by hand for every problem (which a full signals course does cover), control engineering in practice relies on a small table of known transform pairs, combined with a handful of properties — the most important of which is what happens to a derivative.

Why differentiation becomes multiplication by s — the single fact that makes everything work

Integrating f'(t)·e^(−st) by parts (a standard calculus technique) produces the property L{f'(t)} = s·F(s) − f(0) — and with zero initial conditions (f(0)=0, the standard assumption for transfer functions, as Chapter 2 noted), this simplifies to exactly L{f'(t)} = s·F(s). This single property is the entire justification for Chapter 2's "d/dt becomes s" rule — it's not a notational convenience, it's a proven mathematical fact about how the transform interacts with derivatives, which is precisely why applying it to a differential equation converts it into ordinary algebra.

A small table of common transform pairs

Why a step input specifically is the standard test signal

A step input (suddenly switching from 0 to a fixed value, and staying there) is the standard way to characterize a control system's response, because it's the simplest possible "sudden change" — and its transform, 1/s, is about as algebraically simple as a transform gets, making it easy to combine with a plant's transfer function. Physically, a step input models exactly the kind of event a real system needs to handle well: a thermostat's target suddenly changing, a cruise control's target speed being set — a system that responds well to a step input generally responds well to gentler, more gradual changes too.

Section 3.2

Poles and zeros

A transfer function is typically a ratio of two polynomials in s. The zeros are the values of s that make the numerator zero (making G(s)=0); the poles are the values of s that make the denominator zero (making G(s) blow up toward infinity).

G(s) = (s + 2) / ((s + 1)(s + 3))
  Zero at s = −2   (numerator is zero here)
  Poles at s = −1 and s = −3   (denominator is zero here)
Poles are directly analogous to Linear Algebra's eigenvalues

A system's poles play precisely the same role in describing its natural, unforced behavior that eigenvalues play for a matrix in Linear Algebra — both are the specific values that characterize how a system evolves on its own, independent of any particular input. This isn't a loose analogy: for a system described in state-space form (a representation this course doesn't cover in depth, but which underlies more advanced control courses), the poles of the transfer function ARE literally the eigenvalues of the system's state matrix. Recognizing this connection means Linear Algebra's eigenvalue intuition — "these values describe the system's own natural tendencies" — transfers directly into control systems.

Why poles, specifically, determine a system's time-domain behavior

Given a transfer function G(s) and an input X(s), the output Y(s)=G(s)X(s) is converted back to a time-domain response using a technique called partial fraction expansion, which breaks Y(s) into a sum of simple terms, each corresponding to exactly one pole — and, from the table in Section 3.1, each such term (1/(s+a) or similar) transforms back into an exponential term e^(−at) in the time domain. This is precisely why a system's poles directly determine the "shape" of its response: each pole contributes one exponential term to the total time-domain response, and the pole's specific value determines that term's rate of decay (or growth).

A pole's sign determines stability — a preview of Chapter 5

A pole at s=−3 contributes a term like e^(−3t), which decays toward zero as t increases — a stable, well-behaved contribution. A pole at s=+3 would contribute e^(+3t), which grows without bound — an unstable system. Simply glancing at whether all poles have negative real parts is, in fact, exactly Chapter 5's stability criterion in its simplest form — poles are worth understanding well specifically because this single property (their sign) determines whether a system is stable at all, before even considering how well it performs.

Section 3.3

First-order system step response

For the standard first-order form from Chapter 2, G(s) = K/(τs+1), the step response (output when the input is a unit step) is:

y(t) = K × (1 − e^(−t/τ))
This is exactly the RC charging curve — because it IS the RC charging curve

This equation is identical in form to Electronics Chapter 3's capacitor charging formula, because the RC circuit is precisely this standard first-order system, with K=1 (the capacitor eventually reaches the full input voltage) and τ=RC. Nothing new is being computed here — Chapter 3's whole machinery (Laplace transform, poles) has simply provided a general, systematic way to arrive at that same familiar exponential curve for ANY first-order system, not just an RC circuit specifically.

This system has a single pole at s=−1/τ — matching Section 3.2's point directly: one pole, one exponential term, exactly the e^(−t/τ) behavior seen in the step response.

Section 3.4

Second-order system response: overdamped, underdamped, critically damped

Many real systems (including an RLC circuit — Electronics' inductor, resistor, and capacitor together — or a mass-spring-damper mechanical system) are second-order, with a transfer function of the standard form:

G(s) = ωₙ² / (s² + 2ζωₙs + ωₙ²)
  ωₙ = natural frequency (how fast the system would oscillate with NO damping)
  ζ (zeta) = damping ratio (how strongly oscillation is suppressed)
The damping ratio ζ determines the response's entire qualitative character

This second-order system's two poles can be real (both negative, both decaying with no oscillation) or complex (a pair with negative real part, producing decaying oscillation) depending entirely on ζ — directly connecting back to poles determining response shape (Section 3.2). Three named regimes result: overdamped (ζ>1, two distinct real poles, slow smooth approach with no overshoot), critically damped (ζ=1, a repeated real pole, the fastest possible approach with no overshoot), and underdamped (ζ<1, complex conjugate poles, the response oscillates while settling, overshooting the target before settling).

All three reach the same final value, but overdamped is slow with no overshoot, underdamped overshoots and oscillates before settling, and critically damped is the fastest response with no overshoot.
"Fastest" and "no overshoot" aren't always both desirable — this is a real design trade-off

Critically damped sounds ideal (fast, no overshoot), but real designs often deliberately choose a slightly underdamped response, accepting a small overshoot in exchange for reaching the target region faster than critical damping would allow — a genuine trade-off Chapter 6 (PID tuning) makes explicit. Conversely, some applications (positioning a delicate instrument, for instance) cannot tolerate ANY overshoot at all, making overdamped or critically-damped behavior a hard requirement rather than a preference. There's no universally "best" damping ratio — it depends entirely on what the specific application can and can't tolerate.

Section 3.5

Worked problem — predicting response shape from a transfer function

Problem

Given G(s) = 16 / (s² + 4s + 16), find the poles, identify the damping regime, and predict qualitatively how the system responds to a step input.

# Step 1: Match against the standard second-order form
# s² + 2ζωₙs + ωₙ² = s² + 4s + 16
ωₙ² = 16   ->   ωₙ = 4
2ζωₙ = 4   ->   ζ = 4/(2×4) = 0.5

# Step 2: Since ζ = 0.5, and 0 < ζ < 1, this is UNDERDAMPED

# Step 3: Find the poles directly, using the quadratic formula on s²+4s+16=0
s = (−4 ± √(16−64)) / 2 = (−4 ± √(−48)) / 2 = −2 ± j·3.46
# Complex conjugate poles, confirming the underdamped classification from Step 2

# Step 4: Interpret physically
# Real part (−2): decay rate -- how fast the oscillation dies out
# Imaginary part (±3.46): oscillation frequency -- how fast it oscillates while decaying
# Prediction: the output will overshoot the target value, oscillate with decreasing
# amplitude, and settle at the final value after several oscillations

Why the complex poles directly confirm the ζ-based classification, rather than being a separate calculation: Section 3.4 stated that underdamped systems (ζ<1) have complex conjugate poles — Steps 2 and 3 arrive at the same conclusion through two independent routes (the damping ratio formula, and directly solving the quadratic), which is exactly the kind of cross-check worth doing when learning to read transfer functions: if the two methods disagreed, that would flag an arithmetic error.

Why the imaginary part of a complex pole corresponds to oscillation, and the real part to decay: a complex pole always comes in a conjugate pair (a±jb), and standard Laplace-transform algebra shows this pair combines into a real time-domain term of the form e^(at)·sin(bt+φ) — precisely an oscillation (from the sin term, at frequency b) with an exponentially changing envelope (from e^(at), decaying if a is negative). This is the general mechanism behind every underdamped response's oscillate-while-settling shape, not a coincidence specific to this one example.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. The Laplace transform's key property, L{f'(t)}=sF(s) (with zero initial conditions), is the actual mathematical justification for "d/dt becomes s" — not just a convenient shorthand.
  2. A step input (transform 1/s) is the standard test signal for characterizing control system response, since it represents the simplest possible sudden change.
  3. Poles (denominator roots) determine a system's time-domain response shape — directly analogous to Linear Algebra's eigenvalues — with each pole contributing one exponential term; zeros (numerator roots) exist but matter less for basic response shape.
  4. A pole's sign determines stability (negative real part = decaying/stable), previewing Chapter 5 directly.
  5. Second-order systems are classified by damping ratio ζ: overdamped (ζ>1, slow, no overshoot), critically damped (ζ=1, fastest with no overshoot), underdamped (ζ<1, complex poles, oscillates while settling) — no single regime is universally "best"; it depends on what a specific application can tolerate.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

What mathematical property of the Laplace transform actually justifies treating d/dt as multiplication by s?

The property L{f'(t)} = sF(s) - f(0), derived from integration by parts. With the standard zero-initial-condition assumption (f(0)=0), this simplifies to L{f'(t)} = sF(s) -- a proven mathematical fact, not just a notational convenience, which is why applying the Laplace transform converts a differential equation into ordinary algebra.
Question 2

Why does a pole with a positive real part indicate an unstable system?

Each pole contributes an exponential term of the form e^(pole×t) to the time-domain response. A pole with a positive real part produces a term that grows without bound as t increases, rather than decaying toward zero -- meaning the system's response grows unboundedly over time, which is the definition of instability.
Question 3

Why does an underdamped system's step response oscillate while settling, rather than approaching the target smoothly?

An underdamped system (ζ<1) has complex conjugate poles. This pole pair combines mathematically into a time-domain term shaped like e^(at)·sin(bt+φ) -- the sin term produces oscillation at a frequency set by the poles' imaginary part, while the exponential term (from the poles' real part) shrinks that oscillation's amplitude over time, producing the characteristic overshoot-then-settle pattern.

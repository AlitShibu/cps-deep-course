Block Diagram Algebra & Feedback

Chapter 1 drew feedback loops as pictures; Chapters 2-3 gave you transfer functions and the tools to read their behavior. This chapter connects them: a formal method for reducing any block diagram — however many blocks and feedback loops it contains — down to one single transfer function, and the specific formula for a feedback loop that turns Chapter 1's qualitative "feedback corrects for disturbances" claim into something you can actually calculate.

Section 4.1

Reducing series and parallel blocks

SERIES (cascaded) blocks:  X -> [G1(s)] -> [G2(s)] -> Y
  Combined:  G(s) = G1(s) × G2(s)

PARALLEL blocks (same input, outputs summed):
  X -> [G1(s)] -\
                  (+) -> Y
  X -> [G2(s)] -/
  Combined:  G(s) = G1(s) + G2(s)
Why series blocks simply multiply — this is just function composition

If Y1(s)=G1(s)X(s) and Y(s)=G2(s)Y1(s), substituting the first into the second gives Y(s)=G2(s)G1(s)X(s) directly — series combination is nothing more than function composition, expressed as multiplication because transfer functions are algebraic ratios rather than the differential equations they represent. This is exactly why the s-domain reformulation from Chapter 2 was worth the abstraction: combining two systems in the time domain would require substituting one differential equation into another, a considerably messier operation than simple multiplication.

Section 4.2

The closed-loop transfer function

For the standard negative-feedback structure from Chapter 1's diagram — a forward path G(s) and a feedback path H(s), with the feedback signal subtracted from the reference — the closed-loop transfer function has a specific, named form:

The general negative-feedback structure: forward path G(s), feedback path H(s) measuring/scaling the output before it's subtracted from the reference.
T(s) = Y(s)/R(s) = G(s) / (1 + G(s)H(s))
Deriving this formula directly from the diagram (worth doing once, by hand)

Let E(s) be the summing junction's output (the error signal): E(s) = R(s) − H(s)Y(s). The forward path gives Y(s) = G(s)E(s). Substituting: Y(s) = G(s)[R(s) − H(s)Y(s)] = G(s)R(s) − G(s)H(s)Y(s). Collecting all Y(s) terms on one side: Y(s) + G(s)H(s)Y(s) = G(s)R(s), so Y(s)[1+G(s)H(s)] = G(s)R(s), giving T(s)=Y(s)/R(s)=G(s)/(1+G(s)H(s)) directly. This is genuinely just algebra — two equations, one substitution, one factoring step — and deriving it once by hand (rather than only memorizing the final formula) makes it far easier to correctly adapt when a specific diagram doesn't exactly match this standard picture.

Unity feedback (H(s)=1) is extremely common — and easy to mishandle

When the feedback path is simply a direct wire (the sensor perfectly and instantly reports the true output, with no scaling or dynamics of its own), H(s)=1, and the formula simplifies to T(s)=G(s)/(1+G(s)) — a form you'll see constantly. A common mistake is forgetting that H(s) is still conceptually present (just equal to 1) and, for instance, incorrectly substituting a sensor's actual transfer function into the wrong place in a more complex diagram — always identify exactly what closes the loop back to the summing junction before applying the formula.

Section 4.3

Making Chapter 1's feedback claim quantitative

Chapter 1 argued qualitatively that feedback corrects for model uncertainty and disturbances. The closed-loop formula makes this precise: consider a plant whose true gain G differs somewhat from the design's assumed value, due to manufacturing tolerance or an imperfect model.

Why large loop gain (G(s)H(s)) suppresses sensitivity to G's own variation

If G(s)H(s) is large compared to 1, the closed-loop transfer function T(s)=G/(1+GH) approaches G/(GH) = 1/H — notice G has essentially canceled out of the expression. This is a genuinely important, non-obvious result: with sufficiently large loop gain, the overall closed-loop behavior becomes governed almost entirely by the feedback path H(s) (often just a sensor's characteristics, which can be made very precise and stable) rather than by the plant G(s)'s own gain (which may be poorly known or variable) — precisely the mathematical mechanism behind Chapter 1's claim that feedback compensates for model uncertainty, now derived rather than asserted.

This is exactly why Electronics Chapter 6's op-amp gain formula ignores the op-amp's own gain

Electronics Chapter 6 stated that an op-amp's closed-loop gain (like −R_f/R_in) depends only on external resistor values, not on the op-amp's own enormous, imprecise open-loop gain — this is precisely the large-loop-gain approximation just derived, applied to that specific circuit. The op-amp's open-loop gain plays the role of G(s) here, is enormous (satisfying "GH large"), and correspondingly vanishes from the final closed-loop expression — the resistor network (H(s), effectively) is what's left determining the actual behavior. What looked like a specific circuit fact in Electronics Chapter 6 turns out to be one instance of this chapter's general feedback principle.

Section 4.4

Worked problem — reducing a two-block feedback system

Problem

A DC motor speed control system has a controller C(s)=5 (a simple proportional gain), a motor plant G(s)=2/(s+2) (from Chapter 2's first-order motor model), and unity feedback (H(s)=1, an ideal speed sensor). Find the overall closed-loop transfer function T(s)=Ω(s)/R(s).

# Step 1: Combine the controller and plant in series (Section 4.1) --
# they act as one effective forward-path block
G_forward(s) = C(s) × G(s) = 5 × 2/(s+2) = 10/(s+2)

# Step 2: Apply the closed-loop feedback formula (Section 4.2), with H(s)=1
T(s) = G_forward(s) / (1 + G_forward(s)·H(s))
T(s) = [10/(s+2)] / (1 + 10/(s+2))

# Step 3: Simplify -- multiply numerator and denominator by (s+2) to clear the fraction
T(s) = 10 / [(s+2) + 10]
T(s) = 10 / (s + 12)

# Step 4: Rewrite in standard first-order form (Chapter 2/3) by dividing through by 12
T(s) = (10/12) / ((1/12)s + 1) = 0.833 / (0.0833s + 1)
  New DC gain ≈ 0.833
  New time constant ≈ 0.0833 seconds

Comparing to the plant alone: the uncontrolled motor G(s)=2/(s+2), rewritten in standard form, has DC gain 1 and time constant 0.5 seconds. The closed-loop system responds roughly 6× faster (time constant dropped from 0.5s to 0.083s) — directly demonstrating a genuine, calculable benefit of feedback: proportional control here doesn't just correct errors, it measurably speeds up the system's response, a concrete, numeric instance of exactly the kind of improvement Chapter 1 promised only qualitatively.

Why the DC gain changed from 1 to 0.833, and why that matters: the closed-loop system doesn't reach the exact reference value at steady state — a reference of 1 produces a steady-state output of only 0.833, a persistent steady-state error. This is a genuine limitation of simple proportional control that Chapter 6 (PID) directly addresses with the integral term, specifically introduced to drive steady-state error to zero — this worked problem's imperfect DC gain is exactly the kind of shortfall that motivates PID's more sophisticated structure.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. Series blocks multiply (function composition); parallel blocks add — both are algebraic operations only because the s-domain reformulation (Chapter 2) replaced differential-equation substitution with simple arithmetic.
  2. The closed-loop transfer function, T(s)=G(s)/(1+G(s)H(s)), can be derived directly from the summing-junction and forward-path equations — worth deriving by hand once rather than only memorizing.
  3. When loop gain G(s)H(s) is large, T(s)≈1/H(s) — the closed-loop behavior becomes governed by the (often precise, stable) feedback path rather than the (often uncertain) plant gain. This is the precise mathematical mechanism behind Chapter 1's qualitative feedback claims, and behind Electronics Chapter 6's op-amp gain formula specifically.
  4. Combining a controller and plant in series, then closing the loop, can measurably change both the system's time constant (speed of response) and DC gain — feedback's benefits are calculable, not just qualitative.
  5. Simple proportional control often leaves a persistent steady-state error (closed-loop DC gain ≠ 1) — directly motivating the integral term in PID control, covered in Chapter 6.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why do two blocks in series combine by simple multiplication of their transfer functions?

Series combination is function composition: if Y1=G1(s)X and Y=G2(s)Y1, substituting gives Y=G2(s)G1(s)X directly. Because transfer functions are algebraic ratios (thanks to the Laplace transform), this composition becomes ordinary multiplication rather than substituting one differential equation into another.
Question 2

Why does a large loop gain G(s)H(s) make the closed-loop system's behavior depend mainly on H(s) rather than G(s)?

When G(s)H(s) is large compared to 1, T(s)=G/(1+GH) approaches G/(GH) = 1/H -- the G term effectively cancels out of the expression. This means the plant's own gain, which may be uncertain or variable, has little influence on the overall closed-loop behavior, while the feedback path (often a precise, stable sensor) dominates -- exactly the mechanism behind feedback's robustness to model uncertainty.
Question 3

In the worked problem, why does the closed-loop system have a steady-state error even though the controller successfully sped up the response?

The closed-loop DC gain came out to 0.833, not exactly 1 -- meaning for a reference of 1, the steady-state output settles at only 0.833, not the full reference value. This is a genuine limitation of simple proportional control (a fixed gain multiplying the error), which the integral term in PID control (Chapter 6) is specifically designed to eliminate.

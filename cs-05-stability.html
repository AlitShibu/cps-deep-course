Stability

Chapter 1's pitfall box warned that a poorly designed feedback controller can make a system's behavior worse than no control at all — even unstable. Chapter 3 hinted at why, through pole signs. This chapter makes that precise: a formal definition of stability, the geometric intuition of the s-plane, and the Routh-Hurwitz criterion — a method for determining stability without ever needing to actually solve for a system's poles, which matters enormously once a characteristic equation is too complex to factor by hand.

Chapter 5 of 7Builds on Chapters 3–4The most consequential chapter in this topic — an unstable system is a failed design
Section 5.1

What stability means, formally

A system is BIBO stable (Bounded-Input, Bounded-Output) if every bounded input (one that never grows without limit) produces a bounded output. This is a deliberately strict, practical definition — a system that produces a merely large-but-finite output for a reasonable input is stable; a system whose output grows without bound (even slowly) for any bounded input is not.

Why stability is checked first, before anything else about a design

An unstable closed-loop system isn't a design that "performs poorly" — it's a system whose output diverges, growing until something physically breaks, saturates, or the system destroys itself (a motor spinning faster and faster until it fails, a robotic arm oscillating with increasing violence). Every other design consideration in this topic — response speed, overshoot, steady-state error — is meaningless to discuss for a system that isn't even stable in the first place, which is exactly why stability is checked before optimizing anything else, and why Chapter 1 treated instability as a genuine engineering risk of adding feedback carelessly, not a hypothetical edge case.

The stability criterion, restated from Chapter 3, now made the central topic

Chapter 3 showed that each pole contributes a term e^(pole×t) to the time-domain response. A system is BIBO stable if and only if every pole of its transfer function has a strictly negative real part — every term then decays toward zero over time. A pole with a positive real part grows without bound (unstable); a pole exactly on the imaginary axis (zero real part) neither grows nor decays, producing sustained oscillation forever — a borderline case called marginal stability, generally treated as unacceptable in practice since real-world noise or any tiny disturbance can push a marginally stable system into actual instability.

Section 5.2

The s-plane: a geometric picture of stability

Since poles are, in general, complex numbers (Chapter 3's worked problem found a pole pair −2±j3.46), it's standard to plot them on the s-plane — a graph with the real part of s on the horizontal axis and the imaginary part on the vertical axis.

Poles anywhere in the left half of the s-plane (negative real part) mean stability; anywhere in the right half means instability — the imaginary axis itself is the marginal boundary.
The single geometric test that matters

Color and shading in a diagram are just visual aids — the geometric fact that actually matters is entirely about which side of the vertical (imaginary) axis a pole sits on. Every pole strictly to the left of the imaginary axis (negative real part) means stable behavior for that pole's contribution; every pole strictly to the right (positive real part) means unstable, uncontrolled growth. This single geometric test — "which side of the axis" — is the entire content of stability analysis, however complex the underlying transfer function.

Complex poles come in conjugate pairs — always symmetric about the real axis

Because physical systems have real-valued coefficients in their differential equations (and therefore real coefficients in the transfer function's denominator), any complex pole must appear together with its complex conjugate (Chapter 3's −2±j3.46 pair is a direct example) — poles are always symmetric above and below the real axis. This is a useful sanity check: a lone, unpaired complex pole in a supposedly correct calculation signals an arithmetic mistake somewhere, since it couldn't physically occur alone.

Section 5.3

The Routh-Hurwitz criterion

Finding a high-order polynomial's roots explicitly (to check their sign) is often impractical by hand. The Routh-Hurwitz criterion determines how many roots have positive real parts directly from the polynomial's coefficients, without ever solving for the roots themselves.

# For a characteristic equation a₀sⁿ + a₁s^(n-1) + ... + aₙ = 0,
# build the Routh array. For a third-order example, a₀s³ + a₁s² + a₂s + a₃ = 0:

Row s³:   a₀        a₂
Row s²:   a₁        a₃
Row s¹:   (a₁a₂-a₀a₃)/a₁      0
Row s⁰:   a₃

# STABILITY TEST: the system is stable if and only if EVERY entry in the
# first column is the same sign (conventionally, all positive)
Why "first column all same sign" works, without needing to justify the full derivation

The Routh-Hurwitz method is built specifically so the number of sign changes down the first column equals the number of roots with positive real parts — zero sign changes means zero unstable roots, which is exactly the BIBO stability condition from Section 5.1. The full derivation involves a fair amount of polynomial theory beyond this chapter's scope, but the practical takeaway — build the array from the coefficients, check the first column for sign changes — is a completely mechanical, reliable procedure once the pattern is learned, exactly the kind of "trust the established tool, understand what it tells you" approach engineers take with any well-established method.

A necessary (but not sufficient) quick pre-check: all coefficients must be positive

Before building the full Routh array, a fast sanity check: if any coefficient in the characteristic equation is zero or negative (with at least one positive coefficient present), the system is guaranteed unstable — no further calculation needed. This doesn't replace the full Routh-Hurwitz test (all-positive coefficients don't guarantee stability by themselves), but it's a genuinely useful first glance that can save time by immediately flagging an obviously unstable characteristic equation.

Section 5.4

How increasing controller gain can destabilize a system

Chapter 4 showed that increasing loop gain generally speeds up a system's response and reduces sensitivity to plant uncertainty. That's not the whole story — for many real systems, increasing gain far enough eventually pushes one or more closed-loop poles across the imaginary axis into the right half-plane, destabilizing the system entirely.

Why "more gain is always better" is a genuinely dangerous oversimplification

The closed-loop poles are the roots of the characteristic equation 1+G(s)H(s)=0 — as the controller's gain (a factor inside G(s)) increases, these roots move continuously through the s-plane (a relationship formally studied as the "root locus," beyond this chapter's scope but worth knowing by name). For many systems, especially higher-order ones, this movement eventually crosses into the right half-plane at some critical gain value — meaning a controller that works perfectly well at a moderate gain can become genuinely unstable if tuned too aggressively, purely from a well-intentioned attempt to make the response faster. This is precisely why Chapter 6's PID tuning discussion treats "how much gain" as a real design trade-off rather than "more is simply better."

Section 5.5

Worked problem — finding the maximum stable gain

Problem

A system has open-loop transfer function G(s)=K/(s(s+1)(s+2)), with unity feedback. Find the characteristic equation and determine, using Routh-Hurwitz, the maximum value of K for which the closed-loop system remains stable.

# Step 1: The characteristic equation is 1 + G(s)H(s) = 0, with H(s)=1
1 + K/(s(s+1)(s+2)) = 0
s(s+1)(s+2) + K = 0
s³ + 3s² + 2s + K = 0        (expanding s(s+1)(s+2) = s³+3s²+2s)

# Step 2: Build the Routh array (matching Section 5.3's pattern, a₀=1, a₁=3, a₂=2, a₃=K)
Row s³:   1              2
Row s²:   3              K
Row s¹:   (3×2 - 1×K)/3      0
Row s⁰:   K

# Step 3: Apply the stability condition -- every first-column entry must be positive
1 > 0                    ✓ (always true)
3 > 0                    ✓ (always true)
(6-K)/3 > 0   ->   K < 6
K > 0                    ✓ (required separately -- a negative gain is a different problem)

# Step 4: Combine the conditions
0 < K < 6

Why this result is a genuinely useful, actionable design number: this single calculation says a proportional controller for this specific plant can use any gain strictly between 0 and 6 and remain stable — going as high as possible within that range (approaching, but staying below, K=6) generally gives the fastest response the design permits, directly connecting Section 5.4's warning to a concrete number rather than a vague caution. In practice, a real design would stay comfortably below K=6 (a "gain margin," a concept from more advanced stability analysis) rather than pushing right up to the theoretical boundary, since real systems always have some model uncertainty this idealized calculation doesn't capture.

Why K=0 and K=6 are both excluded, not included, in the stable range: at K=6 exactly, the first-column entry (6-K)/3 becomes exactly zero — this is precisely the marginal-stability case from Section 5.1, corresponding to a pole landing exactly on the imaginary axis, producing sustained (not decaying) oscillation. Since marginal stability is treated as practically unacceptable, the boundary itself is excluded, giving a strict inequality rather than K≤6.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. BIBO stability: every bounded input produces a bounded output. A system is stable if and only if every pole has a strictly negative real part; a pole on the imaginary axis is marginally stable (sustained oscillation) — treated as unacceptable in practice.
  2. The s-plane gives a geometric picture: left half-plane poles are stable, right half-plane poles are unstable. Complex poles always occur in conjugate pairs for physical systems.
  3. The Routh-Hurwitz criterion determines stability directly from a characteristic equation's coefficients (via the Routh array's first column) — without ever solving for the roots explicitly, essential once a system is too high-order to factor by hand.
  4. Increasing controller gain generally speeds up response (Chapter 4), but can also push closed-loop poles into the right half-plane at high enough gain — "more gain is better" is a genuine oversimplification, not a safe default.
  5. Routh-Hurwitz can find the exact maximum stable gain for a given system — a concrete, actionable design boundary, though real designs typically stay comfortably below it as a safety margin against model uncertainty.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why is a pole located exactly on the imaginary axis (zero real part) considered only "marginally" stable rather than fully stable?

A pole with zero real part contributes a term that neither grows nor decays over time -- it produces sustained oscillation forever, rather than settling. This is treated as practically unacceptable because any small real-world disturbance or noise can push such a system into genuine, growing instability, unlike a pole with a strictly negative real part which reliably decays.
Question 2

What does the Routh-Hurwitz criterion let you determine that would otherwise require finding a polynomial's roots directly?

It determines whether all of a characteristic equation's roots have negative real parts (i.e., whether the system is stable) purely from the polynomial's coefficients, by checking for sign changes in the first column of the Routh array -- without ever needing to solve for the actual root values, which matters greatly for higher-order polynomials that are impractical to factor by hand.
Question 3

Why can increasing a controller's gain, which generally speeds up a system's response, also cause instability?

The closed-loop poles are roots of the characteristic equation 1+G(s)H(s)=0, and increasing the gain (part of G(s)) moves these roots continuously through the s-plane. For many systems, especially higher-order ones, this movement can eventually cross the imaginary axis into the right half-plane at some critical gain value, destabilizing a system that was perfectly stable at lower gain.

PID Control

Chapter 4's worked problem used a simple proportional controller and found a persistent steady-state error. This chapter introduces the two additional terms — integral and derivative — that, combined with proportional, form PID control: the single most widely used control structure in real industrial and embedded systems, precisely because it directly addresses proportional control's shortcomings while remaining simple enough to actually implement on a microcontroller.

Section 6.1

The proportional term: reacting to current error

u(t) = Kp × e(t)
  u(t) = controller output (the signal sent to the plant)
  e(t) = error = reference − measured output
  Kp = proportional gain
Proportional control is exactly Chapter 4's worked problem, generalized

Chapter 4's controller, C(s)=5, was precisely a proportional controller with Kp=5 — "apply an output proportional to however large the current error is." Larger Kp means a stronger, faster-reacting correction for the same error, which is exactly why increasing Kp sped up the closed-loop response in that worked problem. But that same worked problem's finding — a closed-loop DC gain of 0.833, not 1 — reveals proportional control's fundamental limitation directly: a non-zero error is required to produce any non-zero output at all, so at steady state, some residual error must persist to keep sustaining whatever output the plant needs to hold its position.

Increasing Kp to shrink steady-state error runs directly into Chapter 5's stability limit

Since a larger Kp reduces (but never entirely eliminates) proportional-only steady-state error, it's tempting to simply keep increasing it — but Chapter 5's worked problem showed exactly why this fails: past some critical gain, the closed-loop system becomes unstable. Proportional control alone faces a genuine, unavoidable trade-off between steady-state accuracy and stability margin, which is precisely the gap the integral term exists to close without needing ever-larger proportional gain.

Section 6.2

The integral term: eliminating steady-state error

u(t) = Kp×e(t) + Ki × ∫₀ᵗ e(τ)dτ
  Ki = integral gain
  The integral term accumulates error OVER TIME
Why accumulating error over time drives steady-state error to exactly zero

As long as ANY error persists, even a tiny one, the integral term keeps growing (accumulating that error over time), continuing to increase the controller's output — this growth only stops once the error reaches exactly zero, since only then does the integral stop accumulating and settle at a constant value. This is a fundamentally different mechanism than proportional control: rather than needing a non-zero error to sustain a non-zero output (P's limitation from Section 6.1), the integral term can hold a steady non-zero output indefinitely, with zero error, purely from what it accumulated in the past. This is precisely why adding an integral term drives steady-state error to zero for a step reference — exactly Chapter 4's shortfall, now resolved.

Integral windup: a genuinely important real-world failure mode

If a large, sustained error persists for a while (e.g. a plant physically can't respond fast enough, or an actuator is saturated at its maximum output), the integral term keeps accumulating and can grow to an enormous value — once the error finally starts to correct, this "wound up" integral term keeps driving a large output long after it should have backed off, causing significant overshoot. Practical PID implementations almost universally include anti-windup logic (commonly: simply clamping the integral term's accumulated value within reasonable bounds) specifically to prevent this — a textbook PID formula alone, implemented naively, is a known, common source of real-world control misbehavior.

Section 6.3

The derivative term: anticipating where error is heading

u(t) = Kp×e(t) + Ki∫₀ᵗe(τ)dτ + Kd × (de/dt)
  Kd = derivative gain
  The derivative term reacts to how FAST the error is changing
Why the derivative term specifically counters overshoot

If error is rapidly shrinking (the system is approaching the target quickly), de/dt is a large negative number, and the derivative term contributes a large negative correction — effectively "braking" the controller's output before the error reaches zero, rather than waiting for the error itself to signal a problem. This directly addresses the underdamped, oscillating overshoot behavior from Chapter 3's second-order response discussion: the derivative term acts like additional damping, specifically counteracting the momentum that causes a system to overshoot its target and oscillate.

Derivative action is extremely sensitive to measurement noise

A derivative is fundamentally a rate of change — and small, random noise in a sensor reading (present in essentially every real measurement) produces wildly fluctuating instantaneous rates of change, even when the underlying true signal is smooth. A naive derivative term can amplify this noise into a jittery, erratic controller output, sometimes making performance genuinely worse rather than better. Real implementations commonly apply a low-pass filter (Electronics Chapter 4's RC filter concept, directly) to the signal before differentiating it, trading a small amount of derivative responsiveness for a much more stable, usable output.

Section 6.4

Combining P, I, D: qualitative tuning intuition

Increasing...Typical effect
KpFaster response, reduced (not eliminated) steady-state error, more overshoot, can destabilize at high values
KiEliminates steady-state error, but tends to increase overshoot and can slow settling if too large (risk of windup)
KdReduces overshoot and oscillation (added damping), but amplifies sensor noise if too large
Why manual, iterative tuning is standard practice, not a sign of an incomplete theory

Formal methods exist for choosing Kp/Ki/Kd from a known transfer function (the Ziegler-Nichols method, based on specific response measurements, is the most commonly taught starting point) — but in practice, given real plant uncertainty and non-linearities the LTI model doesn't capture (Chapter 2's stiction/saturation point), engineers very commonly start from a formula-based estimate and then tune iteratively on the real system: increase Kp until response is reasonably fast, add Ki to remove steady-state error, add Kd if overshoot is a problem, and repeat. This isn't an admission that the theory in this chapter doesn't work — it's an acknowledgment that the LTI model, however useful, is always an approximation of a messier physical reality, and real tuning closes that gap.

Section 6.5

Worked problem — implementing discrete PID on a microcontroller

Problem

The continuous PID formula involves an integral and a derivative — neither of which a microcontroller can compute directly on a continuously-sampled digital signal. Derive a practical discrete-time approximation suitable for implementation in code, sampled at a fixed interval.

# The integral, approximated as a running sum (Riemann sum, sampled every dt seconds):
integral += error × dt

# The derivative, approximated as the change in error since the last sample:
derivative = (error - previous_error) / dt

# Full discrete PID controller, in Arduino-style C (Microcontrollers Chapter 4's
# millis()-based non-blocking timing provides exactly the dt this needs):

float Kp = 2.0, Ki = 0.5, Kd = 0.1;
float integral = 0, previousError = 0;
unsigned long lastTime = 0;

float computePID(float setpoint, float measured) {
    unsigned long now = millis();
    float dt = (now - lastTime) / 1000.0;   // convert ms to seconds
    lastTime = now;

    float error = setpoint - measured;
    integral += error * dt;
    integral = constrain(integral, -100, 100);   // basic anti-windup (Section 6.2)
    float derivative = (error - previousError) / dt;
    previousError = error;

    return Kp*error + Ki*integral + Kd*derivative;
}

Why this connects directly back to Microcontrollers Chapter 4: computing dt from millis() on each call is precisely the non-blocking timing pattern from that chapter — this PID loop is meant to be called repeatedly inside loop() (or on a fixed hardware timer interrupt, for more precise, jitter-free dt), never inside a delay()-blocked section, since a PID controller fundamentally needs to keep sampling and correcting continuously to do its job.

Why the anti-windup clamp is not optional, even in a "simple" implementation: without the constrain() line, a sustained large error (a motor that can't yet reach its target speed, for instance) would let integral grow without bound — precisely Section 6.2's windup failure mode, described there in the abstract and now shown as a single missing line of code away from actually happening in a real embedded implementation.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. Proportional (P) control reacts to current error but requires persistent error to sustain non-zero output — the direct cause of Chapter 4's steady-state error finding. Increasing Kp reduces this error but risks Chapter 5's instability.
  2. Integral (I) control accumulates error over time, eliminating steady-state error entirely (it can sustain output with zero present error) — but risks windup during large, sustained errors without anti-windup protection.
  3. Derivative (D) control reacts to the error's rate of change, adding damping that reduces overshoot — but is highly sensitive to sensor noise, usually requiring a low-pass filter (Electronics Ch.4) before differentiating.
  4. PID tuning combines formula-based starting estimates (e.g. Ziegler-Nichols) with iterative real-system adjustment, since the LTI model is always an approximation of a messier physical reality.
  5. A practical microcontroller PID implementation discretizes the integral (running sum × dt) and derivative (difference ÷ dt), uses Chapter 4-style non-blocking timing for dt, and includes an anti-windup clamp as a near-mandatory safeguard, not an optional refinement.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why can proportional control alone never fully eliminate steady-state error?

Proportional control's output is u(t)=Kp×e(t) -- a non-zero output requires a non-zero error, since the output is directly proportional to it. At steady state, if the plant needs a sustained non-zero input to hold its position, some residual error must persist just to keep producing that required output; only a zero output could correspond to zero error, which isn't generally what the plant needs.
Question 2

Why does the integral term succeed at eliminating steady-state error where the proportional term fails?

The integral term accumulates error over time and can hold a constant, non-zero output value indefinitely based purely on what it accumulated in the past, even once the current error reaches exactly zero. Unlike proportional control, it doesn't need an ongoing non-zero error to sustain a non-zero output, which is exactly why adding it drives steady-state error to zero.
Question 3

Why is a low-pass filter commonly applied before computing the derivative term in a real PID implementation?

A derivative is a rate of change, and small random noise present in essentially any real sensor reading produces wildly fluctuating instantaneous rates of change even when the true underlying signal is smooth. Differentiating a noisy signal directly can amplify this noise into an erratic, jittery controller output; filtering the signal first (an RC-style low-pass filter) smooths out the noise before it gets amplified by the derivative calculation.

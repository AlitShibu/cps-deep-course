Number Systems & Binary Representation

Every digital circuit you'll build in this course — gates, flip-flops, counters — ultimately manipulates just two voltage levels. Before touching any circuit, you need complete fluency with the number system that describes them: binary, and the hexadecimal shorthand engineers actually use day to day when binary gets unwieldy.

Section 1.1

Why computers use binary at all

A digital circuit needs to reliably distinguish between states using real, physical voltage. Distinguishing ten different voltage levels reliably (to directly represent decimal digits 0-9) is hard — tiny amounts of electrical noise could easily blur one level into its neighbor. Distinguishing just two — "high" (close to the supply voltage, e.g. 5V or 3.3V) and "low" (close to 0V) — is comparatively easy to build reliably, even in the presence of significant electrical noise.

Two wide, well-separated voltage zones tolerate far more noise than ten narrow ones would.

Every digital gate you'll meet in Chapter 2 is fundamentally a tiny circuit that reads these two-level inputs and produces a two-level output — and every number, character, or instruction inside a computer is, underneath, nothing but a sequence of these high/low states, interpreted according to some convention. This chapter is entirely about that convention: how a sequence of 1s and 0s represents a number.

Section 1.2

Binary — base 2, from first principles

Decimal (base 10) uses ten digits (0-9), and each position's value is a power of 10: the number present at position i gives digit×10i. Binary (base 2) uses exactly the same idea with only two digits (0, 1), and each position's value is a power of 2 instead.

Decimal 237:
  2×100 + 3×10 + 7×1  =  237

Binary 11101101:
  1×128 + 1×64 + 1×32 + 0×16 + 1×8 + 1×4 + 0×2 + 1×1
=   128  +  64  +  32  +  0  +  8  +  4  +  0  +  1
=  237
Each bit position doubles in value moving left, exactly like each decimal digit is worth 10x its right neighbor.
Worked problem — decimal to binary conversion

Problem: Convert 45 to binary, using repeated division by 2.

45 ÷ 2 = 22 remainder 1
22 ÷ 2 = 11 remainder 0
11 ÷ 2 =  5 remainder 1
 5 ÷ 2 =  2 remainder 1
 2 ÷ 2 =  1 remainder 0
 1 ÷ 2 =  0 remainder 1

Reading remainders BOTTOM to TOP: 101101

Verify: 32+0+8+4+0+1 = 45. ✓ Reading remainders bottom-to-top (rather than top-to-bottom) is the one detail that trips people up — the first remainder computed is actually the least significant bit, since it represents "is 45 odd or even," the smallest-scale piece of information.

Section 1.3

Hexadecimal — binary's practical shorthand

Writing out long binary strings by hand is tedious and error-prone — 11101101 is hard to read at a glance. Hexadecimal (base 16) groups binary into chunks of 4 bits at a time, using digits 0-9 and letters A-F (representing 10-15) for each chunk — and it's the format you'll see constantly in datasheets, memory addresses, and register values throughout your Microcontrollers and embedded work.

Binary (4 bits)Hex digitDecimal
000000
100199
1010A10
1111F15
Why exactly 4 bits per hex digit — not an arbitrary choice

4 bits can represent exactly 16 distinct values (2⁴=16), matching hexadecimal's 16 digits (0-9, A-F) precisely — this is why the grouping works out evenly with zero leftover bits, unlike grouping into, say, groups of 3 (which would need an 8-digit system, octal, still used occasionally but far less common in modern embedded work). This exact correspondence is also why hex is the practical middle ground: far more compact than binary for a human to read, but trivially convertible back to binary in your head, 4 bits at a time, unlike decimal which requires real arithmetic to convert.

Worked problem — binary to hex, the fast way

Problem: Convert 11101101 to hexadecimal.

Group into 4-bit chunks, from the RIGHT:
1110 1101

Convert each group independently:
1110 = 14 = E
1101 = 13 = D

Result: 0xED  (the "0x" prefix is the standard C/most-languages convention for "this is hex")

This matches C Chapter 2's <stdint.h> types directly — a register value like 0xFF is instantly recognizable as "all 8 bits set to 1" once you can convert hex to binary in your head this fluently, without needing a calculator every time.

Quick reference: powers of 2 worth memorizing

2^0=1   2^1=2   2^2=4   2^3=8   2^4=16   2^5=32   2^6=64   2^7=128   2^8=256
Section 1.4

Binary arithmetic — addition and the carry

Binary addition follows the same "add and carry" logic as decimal — you just carry at 2 instead of at 10.

Decimal:  Binary:
   27        11011
 + 14      + 01110
 ----      -------
   41        101001

Bit by bit (right to left):
1+0 = 1
1+1 = 10  -> write 0, carry 1
0+1+1(carry) = 10 -> write 0, carry 1
1+1+1(carry) = 11 -> write 1, carry 1
1+0+1(carry) = 10 -> write 0, carry 1
(nothing left) + 1(carry) = 1

Result: 101001 = 32+8+1 = 41 ✓
Why this matters for Chapter 3 and beyond

This exact bit-by-bit addition process — with its carry logic — is precisely what Chapter 3's adder circuits (half adders, full adders) physically implement in hardware, gate by gate. Understanding this arithmetic by hand first makes the upcoming circuit designs feel like a direct, mechanical translation of something you already know, rather than a new abstract idea to learn from scratch.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. Digital circuits use binary (two voltage levels) because distinguishing two well-separated states reliably, even with electrical noise, is far easier than distinguishing ten.
  2. Binary place values are powers of 2, exactly mirroring decimal's powers of 10 — the rightmost bit is the least significant (worth 1), doubling leftward.
  3. Converting decimal to binary: repeatedly divide by 2, reading the remainders bottom to top.
  4. Hexadecimal groups binary into 4-bit chunks (since 4 bits = 16 values = hex's 16 digits exactly) — the practical, compact format used throughout datasheets, memory addresses, and register values.
  5. Binary addition carries at 2 instead of 10, but otherwise follows identical logic to decimal addition — and this bit-by-bit process is exactly what adder circuits (Chapter 3) implement in hardware.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why do digital circuits use binary rather than, say, base 10, even though base 10 would need fewer digits to represent the same number?

Reliably distinguishing ten different voltage levels is much harder in the presence of real-world electrical noise than distinguishing just two well-separated levels (high and low). Binary trades more digits for dramatically more reliable, noise-tolerant circuits.
Question 2

Why does exactly 4 bits correspond to exactly one hexadecimal digit, with no leftover or wasted values?

4 bits can represent exactly 2^4 = 16 distinct values, which exactly matches hexadecimal's 16 digits (0-9 and A-F) -- a perfect one-to-one correspondence with nothing left over.
Question 3

Convert binary 10110 to decimal.

16+0+4+2+0 = 22.

Voltage, Current, Resistance & Ohm's Law

Everything in this topic — and everything downstream of it, from reading a microcontroller datasheet to debugging a circuit that won't power on — rests on three quantities and one equation relating them. This chapter builds voltage, current, and resistance up from the physics of moving charge, rather than presenting Ohm's Law as an equation to memorize, so the "why" behind circuit behavior is available whenever you need it, not just the "what."

Section 1.1

Charge, current, and voltage

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter, measured in coulombs (C). Electrons carry a small negative charge; protons carry an equal, positive charge. Ordinary matter has roughly equal numbers of each and is electrically neutral overall — circuits work by deliberately creating an imbalance and letting it even out through a controlled path.

Current — charge in motion

Current is the rate at which charge flows past a point, measured in amperes (A), where 1 A means 1 coulomb of charge passing per second:

I = Q / t
  I = current (amperes, A)
  Q = charge (coulombs, C)
  t = time (seconds, s)
Conventional current vs. electron flow

In a metal wire, it's actually negatively-charged electrons that physically move, and they move from the negative terminal toward the positive terminal. But circuit conventions — set before electrons were even discovered — define conventional current as flowing from positive to negative, the opposite direction. Every schematic, every formula, every arrow you'll see in this course uses conventional current. It's not "wrong" that electrons go the other way; it's simply a different, and universally adopted, bookkeeping convention. Get comfortable with conventional current now — fighting it later causes real confusion.

Voltage — the "push" behind current

Current doesn't flow on its own — it needs a driving force, analogous to pressure driving water through a pipe. That driving force is voltage (also called potential difference), measured in volts (V). Voltage is always a difference between two points — there's no such thing as "the voltage" at a single isolated point without reference to another point (usually ground, covered in Section 1.4).

The water-pipe analogy (useful, but know its limit)

Voltage is like water pressure, current is like the flow rate (liters/second), and resistance (next section) is like a narrow section of pipe restricting flow. Higher pressure pushes more flow through the same restriction; a narrower restriction reduces flow for the same pressure. This analogy is genuinely useful for building intuition, but it breaks down for AC circuits and semiconductor behavior (Chapters 4–5) — treat it as a first mental model, not a literal description.

V = W / Q
  V = voltage (volts, V)
  W = work/energy done moving the charge (joules, J)
  Q = charge (coulombs, C)

One volt is the potential difference that does one joule of work moving one coulomb of charge between two points. This definition is why voltage is fundamentally an energy-per-charge quantity — it's what makes "voltage source" a meaningful idea: something that supplies energy to push charge around a circuit.

Section 1.2

Resistance and Ohm's Law

Resistance is a material's opposition to current flow, measured in ohms (Ω). Every real conductor has some resistance — even a copper wire, though usually small enough to ignore in basic analysis. A resistor is a component manufactured specifically to provide a known, deliberate amount of resistance.

Where resistance physically comes from

As electrons move through a material, they collide with the material's atoms, losing some energy as heat with each collision — this is the microscopic origin of resistance. Materials with electrons that move relatively freely (copper, silver, gold) have low resistance and are called conductors; materials that strongly impede electron movement (rubber, glass, most plastics) have very high resistance and are called insulators. This same collision mechanism is also why resistors get warm, and it foreshadows the power-dissipation discussion in Section 1.3.

Ohm's Law

For most materials at a given temperature, current is directly proportional to voltage — twice the voltage across the same resistance drives twice the current. This relationship is Ohm's Law:

The Ohm's Law triangle: cover the quantity you want to find, and the remaining two show the operation — cover V to see I×R, cover I to see V/R, cover R to see V/I.
V = I × R
I = V / R
R = V / I

  V = voltage across the resistor (volts, V)
  I = current through the resistor (amperes, A)
  R = resistance (ohms, Ω)
Ohm's Law applies across a single component, not "in general"

V, I, and R in the equation must all refer to the same component or the same section of a circuit — the voltage across a specific resistor, and the current through that same resistor. A very common beginner mistake is plugging in the total circuit voltage with one resistor's resistance when other components are also in the circuit. Chapter 2 (series and parallel circuits) is largely about correctly figuring out which voltage goes with which resistance when a circuit has more than one component.

Reading resistor values: the color code

Fixed resistors are usually too small to print numbers on legibly, so they use a standardized color-band code instead. A common 4-band resistor: the first two bands are significant digits, the third is a multiplier (power of ten), and the fourth is a tolerance.

Yellow - Violet - Red - Gold
   4        7      ×100    ±5%
  = 47 × 100 = 4700 Ω = 4.7 kΩ, ±5% tolerance

You won't need to memorize the full color chart for this course, but recognizing that resistor values are read this way — and that a multimeter (Chapter 7) can always confirm a value directly — matters for any hands-on work.

Section 1.3

Power dissipation

Power is the rate at which energy is used or converted, measured in watts (W), where 1 W = 1 joule per second. In a resistor, electrical energy is converted into heat — this is the same collision process from Section 1.2, now expressed as a rate.

P = V × I

Combined with Ohm's Law (V = IR), this can be rewritten two more useful ways:

P = I² × R        (useful when you know current and resistance)
P = V² / R        (useful when you know voltage and resistance)
Why three equivalent forms of the power equation matter in practice

In real problems you rarely know all three of V, I, and R at once — often you know two, and Ohm's Law gives you the third, but it's faster to substitute directly into whichever power formula only needs the two quantities you already have. If you know a resistor's value and the current through it, P = I²R gets you power without a separate step to find voltage first.

Exceeding a resistor's power rating causes real, physical failure

Every resistor has a maximum power rating (commonly ¼ W or ½ W for small through-hole resistors) — exceeding it causes overheating, and in the worst case the resistor burns out or even scorches the board. This isn't a theoretical concern: it's one of the most common ways a hobbyist or student circuit fails, especially LED circuits where a missing or undersized current-limiting resistor lets far more current through than intended. Always check P = I²R against the component's rated power before assuming a resistor value is safe to use.

Section 1.4

Reading a circuit diagram

A schematic represents a circuit's connections and components using standardized symbols — it deliberately ignores physical layout (how components are actually arranged on a board) and shows only what's electrically connected to what.

A minimal complete circuit: a voltage source (battery) and a resistor, connected in a closed loop. Current only flows in a closed path — an open circuit (a break anywhere) means zero current everywhere in that loop.
Why circuits must be a closed loop

Current is charge in continuous motion — for it to flow anywhere, it needs a complete path back to where it started. A break anywhere in the loop (a disconnected wire, an open switch, a blown fuse) stops current everywhere in that loop, not just at the break itself. This is the electrical reason a single burnt-out bulb in an old-style series string of Christmas lights could turn the whole string off.

Ground — the reference point

Since voltage is always a difference between two points, circuits designate one point as ground (0 V reference), and every other voltage in the circuit is quoted relative to it. Ground doesn't have any special physical property — it's a chosen reference, usually the negative terminal of the main power source or a common return path shared by many components.

"Ground" in a circuit diagram isn't always literally connected to the earth

In building wiring, "ground" often does mean a physical earth connection for safety. In a small battery-powered circuit or a microcontroller board, "ground" is just the agreed 0V reference point for that circuit — there's no literal connection to the earth. Both uses share the symbol and the name, which is a common source of confusion for beginners moving between contexts.

Section 1.5

Worked problem — sizing an LED current-limiting resistor

Problem

You want to light an LED from a 5V microcontroller output pin. The LED needs a forward voltage of about 2V and should be driven at 15 mA (0.015 A) for safe, bright operation. What resistor value do you need, and what power rating must it have?

# Step 1: Find the voltage that must be dropped across the RESISTOR
# (not the full 5V -- the LED itself uses 2V of it)
V_resistor = V_source - V_LED = 5V - 2V = 3V

# Step 2: Apply Ohm's Law to the resistor specifically
R = V_resistor / I = 3V / 0.015A = 200 Ω

# Step 3: Check power dissipation in the resistor
P = I² × R = (0.015)² × 200 = 0.045 W

# A standard 1/4 W (0.25 W) resistor comfortably handles 0.045 W --
# a 220 Ω resistor (a common standard value close to 200 Ω) is the typical practical choice

Why Step 1 matters: this is exactly the Section 1.2 pitfall in action — the LED itself consumes 2V of the source's 5V, so only the remaining 3V is actually available to drop across the resistor. Using the full 5V in Ohm's Law here would give the wrong resistor value (too high), under-driving the LED.

Why the power check matters: 0.045 W is comfortably below a standard ¼ W resistor's rating, so a normal small resistor is safe here — but if the design instead called for driving 200 mA through a similarly-sized resistor, the calculated power could exceed a ¼ W part's rating and require a physically larger, higher-rated resistor instead.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. Current (A) is charge flowing per second; voltage (V) is the energy-per-charge "push" driving that flow between two points; conventional current flows from + to −, opposite to actual electron motion.
  2. Resistance (Ω) opposes current flow, arising from electron collisions with atoms in the material. Ohm's Law (V = IR) relates voltage, current, and resistance for a single component — never mix quantities from different components.
  3. Power (W) is the rate of energy conversion: P = VI = I²R = V²/R. Exceeding a resistor's rated power causes real overheating and failure — always check this for any circuit you build.
  4. A schematic shows electrical connections, not physical layout. Current needs a closed loop to flow at all — a break anywhere stops current everywhere in that loop.
  5. Ground is a chosen 0V reference point for a circuit's voltages — in small circuits it's just a convention, not necessarily a literal connection to the earth.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

In a copper wire, electrons physically move from negative to positive. Why do circuit diagrams and formulas still treat current as flowing from positive to negative?

This is "conventional current" -- a bookkeeping convention established before electrons were discovered. It's universally used in schematics and formulas, so it's the direction you should use for circuit analysis, even though it's opposite to the actual direction electrons move.
Question 2

Why is it a mistake to plug a circuit's full source voltage into Ohm's Law with just one resistor's resistance, when the circuit has other components (like an LED) also using up some voltage?

Ohm's Law (V=IR) requires that V, I, and R all refer to the SAME component. If other components (like an LED) also drop some voltage, only the remaining voltage after subtracting those drops is actually available across the resistor. Using the full source voltage would overstate the voltage across the resistor and give an incorrect resistance value.
Question 3

Why does a break anywhere in a circuit's loop stop current everywhere in that loop, not just at the point of the break?

Current is charge in continuous motion around a closed path -- it requires an unbroken loop to flow at all. A break anywhere removes the closed path entirely, so there is no complete circuit for charge to move through anywhere in that loop, not just locally at the break.

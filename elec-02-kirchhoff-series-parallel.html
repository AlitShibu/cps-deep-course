Kirchhoff's Laws, Series & Parallel Circuits

Chapter 1's pitfall box warned that Ohm's Law only applies to a single component at a time — so what do you do with a circuit that has several resistors? This chapter answers that directly: two simple conservation laws (Kirchhoff's), plus the two basic ways components can be wired together (series and parallel), are enough to analyze the large majority of resistor networks you'll ever encounter.

Section 2.1

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law and Current Law

Both of Kirchhoff's laws are, at heart, conservation statements — nothing more exotic than "energy can't appear from nowhere" and "charge can't appear from nowhere," applied to circuits.

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

KVL: the sum of all voltage rises and drops around any closed loop equals zero.

Why KVL must be true

Voltage is energy per unit charge (Chapter 1). If you trace a path all the way around a closed loop and back to your exact starting point, the net energy gained or lost per unit charge must be zero — otherwise a charge could gain energy for free just by going around the loop repeatedly, which would violate conservation of energy. A battery's voltage rise around the loop must exactly equal the sum of all the voltage drops across the resistors it drives.

# A loop with one 9V battery and two resistors in series:
9V (rise, from battery) = V_R1 (drop) + V_R2 (drop)
# i.e. V_R1 + V_R2 = 9V, always -- regardless of the specific resistor values

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

KCL: the total current flowing into any junction (node) equals the total current flowing out of it.

Why KCL must be true

Charge can't accumulate or vanish at a simple wire junction — whatever current arrives has to leave somewhere. If 3A flows into a junction from one wire and the junction splits into two output wires, the two output currents must add up to exactly 3A between them, in whatever proportion the rest of the circuit determines.

KCL: whatever splits at this node, I₁ + I₂ must equal exactly 3A — the specific split depends on the resistances in each branch (Section 2.2).
A useful mental shortcut for both laws

KVL: energy doesn't get created or destroyed going around a loop, so the "ups" and "downs" of voltage must balance. KCL: charge doesn't pile up or vanish at a junction, so what goes in must come out. Every circuit analysis technique you'll ever learn — however complex-looking — ultimately reduces to applying these two ideas systematically.

Section 2.2

Series circuits

Components are in series when they're connected end-to-end along a single path, with no branching — the same current has no choice but to flow through every component in turn.

Why series components share the same current (this is just KCL)

Since there's no branch point between series components, whatever current enters the first one has nowhere else to go except through the next, and the next — by KCL, current can't appear or disappear along a path with no junctions. This is why "series" and "same current throughout" are really the same statement, not two separate facts to memorize.

Total resistance in series

R_total = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...
Why series resistances simply add

Each resistor drops some voltage for the same shared current (Ohm's Law, per component). By KVL, the total voltage supplied must equal the sum of all these individual drops: V_total = I·R1 + I·R2 + I·R3 = I·(R1+R2+R3). Comparing this to Ohm's Law applied to the whole series combination, V_total = I·R_total, shows directly that R_total = R1+R2+R3 — the resistances add because the same current has to overcome each one's opposition in turn.

The voltage divider

Since the same current flows through every series resistor, but each drops a voltage proportional to its own resistance, the source voltage divides up in proportion to resistance — larger resistors take a larger share:

V_R1 = V_total × (R1 / (R1 + R2))
V_R2 = V_total × (R2 / (R1 + R2))

This is one of the single most-used circuits in all of electronics — a voltage divider — used constantly to scale a voltage down to a usable level, for instance reducing a sensor's output voltage to fit within a microcontroller's input range.

Section 2.3

Parallel circuits

Components are in parallel when they're connected across the exact same two nodes — each provides an independent path between those same two points.

Why parallel components share the same voltage (a direct consequence of KVL)

Since every parallel branch connects the same two nodes, and voltage between two specific points is a single, well-defined quantity (Chapter 1), every branch necessarily sees the identical voltage — there's no way for two different paths between the same two points to have a different potential difference between them without violating KVL around the loop formed by those two branches.

Total resistance in parallel

1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + ...

# For exactly TWO resistors, this simplifies to a shortcut worth memorizing:
R_total = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2)
Why parallel resistance is always LESS than the smallest individual resistor

Adding a parallel path gives current an additional route to flow through, which by KCL means more total current flows for the same shared voltage — and more current for the same voltage means, by Ohm's Law, lower overall resistance. This is genuinely counterintuitive at first (adding a resistor decreases total resistance), but it makes sense once you think of it as adding lanes to a highway: more lanes, same "pressure" per lane, more total traffic flow.

The current divider

Since every parallel branch sees the same voltage, but a smaller resistance allows more current for that same voltage, the total current splits in inverse proportion to resistance — more current flows through the smaller-resistance branch:

I_R1 = I_total × (R2 / (R1 + R2))     # note: R1's current depends on R2's value, and vice versa
I_R2 = I_total × (R1 / (R1 + R2))
The current divider formula looks "backwards" at first glance

In the current divider, the current through R1 uses R2 in the numerator, not R1 — the opposite of the voltage divider pattern. This is correct: more current takes the path of least resistance, so R1's share of current is weighted by the other resistor's value. Mixing up voltage-divider and current-divider formulas (using the wrong resistor on top) is one of the most common algebra slips in early circuit analysis — it's worth double-checking which formula applies before plugging in numbers.

Section 2.4

Combined series-parallel networks

Most real circuits are neither purely series nor purely parallel, but a combination of both — the standard analysis strategy is to simplify step by step, collapsing obvious series or parallel groups into a single equivalent resistance, then repeating on what's left.

The general reduction strategy

Scan the circuit for the smallest sub-groups that are purely series or purely parallel, combine each into one equivalent resistor using the formulas from Sections 2.2–2.3, redraw the (now simpler) circuit, and repeat. Eventually the entire network reduces to a single equivalent resistance, at which point Ohm's Law gives the total current directly — then you can work backward through the divider formulas to find the voltage or current at any specific component you actually care about.

Section 2.5

Worked problem — a series-parallel resistor network

Problem

A 12V battery connects to R1 = 4Ω in series with a parallel combination of R2 = 6Ω and R3 = 3Ω. Find the total current from the battery, and the current through R3 specifically.

# Step 1: Reduce the parallel pair (R2 || R3) to one equivalent resistance
R_parallel = (R2 × R3) / (R2 + R3) = (6 × 3) / (6 + 3) = 18 / 9 = 2Ω

# Step 2: Now the circuit is simply R1 in series with this equivalent 2Ω
R_total = R1 + R_parallel = 4 + 2 = 6Ω

# Step 3: Ohm's Law gives the TOTAL current from the battery
I_total = V / R_total = 12V / 6Ω = 2A

# Step 4: This 2A flows through R1 entirely (series -- same current, Section 2.2),
# then splits between R2 and R3 at the parallel junction.
# Find the voltage across the parallel pair first:
V_parallel = I_total × R_parallel = 2A × 2Ω = 4V

# Step 5: R3 sees this full 4V directly (parallel -- same voltage, Section 2.3)
I_R3 = V_parallel / R3 = 4V / 3Ω = 1.33A

Why Step 4's voltage detour was necessary: the current-divider formula from Section 2.3 could have found I_R3 directly (I_R3 = I_total × R2/(R2+R3) = 2 × 6/9 = 1.33A, matching), but computing the shared parallel voltage first is often the more reliable path for a multi-step network — it's a single number that immediately gives you the current through any parallel branch via Ohm's Law, rather than needing a separate divider calculation per branch.

Check with KCL: I_R2 = V_parallel / R2 = 4/6 = 0.67A. Adding the two branch currents: 1.33A + 0.67A = 2.00A, exactly matching I_total — confirming KCL holds at that junction and the arithmetic is consistent.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. KVL: voltage rises and drops around any closed loop sum to zero (energy conservation). KCL: current into a node equals current out of it (charge conservation). Every circuit analysis technique reduces to these two ideas.
  2. Series components share the same current (a direct consequence of KCL with no branch points); resistances simply add: R_total = R1+R2+.... The voltage divider splits voltage in proportion to each resistance.
  3. Parallel components share the same voltage (a direct consequence of KVL between shared nodes); 1/R_total = 1/R1+1/R2+.... Parallel resistance is always less than the smallest branch. The current divider splits current in inverse proportion to resistance (watch the "backwards" numerator).
  4. Combined networks are solved by repeatedly collapsing series/parallel sub-groups into single equivalent resistors until the whole thing reduces to one value.
  5. KCL/KVL double-check your arithmetic: branch currents at a node should sum to the total current in; voltage drops around a loop should sum to the source voltage.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why do components in series necessarily carry the same current?

Series components are connected along a single path with no branch points. By KCL, current can't appear or disappear anywhere along a path with no junctions -- so whatever current enters the first component must be the same current flowing through every subsequent component.
Question 2

Why does adding a resistor in PARALLEL always decrease the total resistance, even though you're adding more resistance to the circuit?

A parallel branch gives current an additional path to flow through. More available paths means more total current can flow for the same shared voltage (by KCL, currents add at the junction). More current for the same voltage means, by Ohm's Law, a lower overall resistance -- even though the new branch itself has nonzero resistance.
Question 3

In the current-divider formula for a two-resistor parallel network, why does the current through R1 use R2 in the numerator rather than R1?

More current flows through the path of least resistance. R1's share of the total current is weighted by the OTHER resistor's value (R2) -- a larger R2 pushes more current toward R1's branch, and vice versa. This is the opposite pattern from the voltage divider, where each resistor's own value appears in its own numerator.

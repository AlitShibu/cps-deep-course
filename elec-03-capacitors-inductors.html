Capacitors, Inductors & RC/RL Transients

Chapters 1–2 dealt only with resistors — components whose behavior doesn't depend on time. This chapter introduces the two other basic passive components, capacitors and inductors, whose defining feature is that they store energy and their behavior genuinely depends on how things are changing over time, not just their instantaneous values. This is the foundation for timing circuits, filters (Chapter 4), and power-supply smoothing (Chapter 7).

Section 3.1

Capacitors — storing energy in an electric field

A capacitor is two conductive plates separated by an insulating material (a dielectric). Applying a voltage across it causes charge to accumulate on the plates — positive on one, negative on the other — creating an electric field between them. This stored charge is what makes a capacitor fundamentally different from a resistor: a resistor dissipates energy as heat, while a capacitor stores it, and can later return it to the circuit.

Q = C × V
  Q = charge stored (coulombs, C)
  C = capacitance (farads, F)
  V = voltage across the capacitor
What capacitance actually measures

Capacitance is a measure of how much charge a capacitor can store per volt applied — larger plates, or plates closer together, or a better dielectric material, all increase capacitance. A farad is a very large unit for most electronics; practical capacitors are usually specified in microfarads (µF, 10⁻⁶F), nanofarads (nF, 10⁻⁹F), or picofarads (pF, 10⁻¹²F).

A capacitor blocks DC once fully charged, but passes AC

Once a capacitor reaches the applied voltage, current through it drops to zero — a fully charged capacitor behaves like an open circuit to steady DC. But if the applied voltage is constantly changing (as in an AC signal), the capacitor is continuously charging and discharging, which looks like current is "passing through" even though no charge actually crosses the insulating gap between the plates. This DC-blocking, AC-passing behavior is exploited constantly in filter design (Chapter 4).

Energy stored in a capacitor

E = ½ × C × V²
  E = energy stored (joules, J)

This stored energy is why capacitors are used for smoothing power supply ripple (Chapter 7) and for brief energy-storage applications like camera flashes — they can release a large amount of stored energy very quickly, far faster than a battery's chemical reactions allow.

Section 3.2

RC transients — charging and discharging

A capacitor doesn't charge or discharge instantly — connecting it to a voltage source through a resistor produces a smooth, exponential change over time, called an RC transient.

V(t) = V_final × (1 − e^(−t/RC))          # charging toward V_final
V(t) = V_initial × e^(−t/RC)               # discharging toward 0

  τ (tau) = R × C     "the time constant," measured in seconds
Charging curve: after one time constant τ, the capacitor reaches ~63% of its final voltage; after roughly 5τ, it's considered "fully" charged (>99%).
Why the time constant is exactly R × C, intuitively

A larger resistance limits the current available to charge the capacitor, slowing the process — so larger R means slower charging (larger τ). A larger capacitance requires more total charge to reach the same voltage (Q=CV), which also takes longer for the same limited current — so larger C also means slower charging. Both effects point the same direction, which is why they multiply together rather than working against each other.

Why "5 time constants" is the practical rule for "fully charged"

The exponential charging curve technically never reaches exactly 100% — it approaches it asymptotically forever. But after 1τ it's at ~63%, after 2τ ~86%, after 3τ ~95%, after 5τ over 99%. In practice, 99% is indistinguishable from "done" for almost any real application, which is why "5τ to fully charge/discharge" is the standard engineering rule of thumb rather than an exact mathematical endpoint.

Section 3.3

Inductors — storing energy in a magnetic field

An inductor is typically a coil of wire. Current flowing through it creates a magnetic field, and that field stores energy — the magnetic counterpart to a capacitor's electric-field storage. Inductance is measured in henries (H).

V = L × (dI/dt)
  V = voltage across the inductor
  L = inductance (henries, H)
  dI/dt = rate of change of current over time
An inductor opposes CHANGE in current, not current itself

Notice the formula depends on how fast current is changing, not on the current's actual value — a steady, unchanging current through an inductor produces zero voltage across it (a fully "charged" inductor looks like a plain wire to steady DC). This is the mirror image of the capacitor: a capacitor opposes change in voltage, an inductor opposes change in current. This symmetry is genuinely useful for remembering both components' behavior together rather than as two unrelated facts.

Inductors resist sudden current changes — and can produce dangerous voltage spikes

If current through an inductor is suddenly interrupted (a switch opening abruptly), dI/dt becomes extremely large for an instant, and V = L·(dI/dt) can spike to a very high voltage — this is exactly the mechanism behind arcing across switch contacts, and why inductive loads (motors, relay coils) in real circuits are almost always paired with a protective diode (a "flyback diode," covered in Chapter 5) to safely dissipate this spike rather than let it damage nearby components.

Energy stored in an inductor

E = ½ × L × I²

Directly parallel to the capacitor's energy formula, with current and inductance in place of voltage and capacitance — another instance of the electric-field/magnetic-field symmetry between these two components.

Section 3.4

RL transients

Just as a resistor-capacitor combination produces an exponential voltage transient, a resistor-inductor combination produces an exponential current transient when a circuit is switched on or off.

I(t) = I_final × (1 − e^(−t/(L/R)))     # current rising toward its final value

  τ (tau) = L / R     "the time constant" for an RL circuit
Why L/R, and why it's "upside down" compared to RC's τ=RC

A larger inductance more strongly opposes a change in current, slowing the rise — so larger L means larger τ, same direction as capacitance in the RC case. But a larger resistance in an RL circuit limits the final steady-state current (Ohm's Law) and also means the circuit reaches that (smaller) target faster for a given inductance — so larger R actually decreases τ here, the opposite relationship from the RC case where larger R slows things down. This is a genuinely easy detail to mix up; the formula itself (τ=L/R vs τ=RC) is worth memorizing directly rather than trying to reason it out from scratch every time.

Capacitors and inductors as circuit "inertia"

A capacitor resists sudden changes in voltage across it — like a heavy object resisting a sudden change in position. An inductor resists sudden changes in current through it — like a heavy object resisting a sudden change in velocity. Neither can change its defining quantity instantaneously; both need time (governed by their respective τ) to respond to a sudden change in the circuit around them.

Section 3.5

Worked problem — RC timing circuit

Problem

A 10kΩ resistor and a 100µF capacitor are in series, connected to a 5V source. How long does it take the capacitor to charge to approximately 3.16V (63% of 5V), and how long until it's considered "fully" charged?

# Step 1: Compute the time constant
τ = R × C = 10,000 Ω × 0.0001 F = 1 second

# Step 2: One time constant is, by definition, when the capacitor reaches ~63% of V_final
# So it reaches ~3.16V after exactly τ = 1 second

# Step 3: "Fully charged" (>99%) by the standard 5τ rule of thumb
t_full ≈ 5 × τ = 5 × 1s = 5 seconds

Why this specific R and C combination is a common design choice: a 1-second time constant from readily available component values (10kΩ, 100µF) makes RC timing circuits a simple, cheap way to build approximate delays without a microcontroller — this exact kind of circuit historically powered basic timer ICs and is still occasionally used for simple debounce or power-on delay circuits in embedded hardware design.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. A capacitor (Q=CV) stores energy in an electric field; it blocks steady DC once charged but allows AC to effectively "pass" through continuous charge/discharge cycles.
  2. An RC transient follows an exponential curve with time constant τ=RC — ~63% after 1τ, considered "fully" charged after ~5τ.
  3. An inductor (V=L·dI/dt) stores energy in a magnetic field and opposes changes in current, not current itself — a steady current sees it as a plain wire. Sudden current interruption can cause dangerous voltage spikes.
  4. An RL transient follows an exponential curve with time constant τ=L/R — note this is inverted relative to RC's τ=RC (larger R decreases RL's τ but increases RC's τ).
  5. Capacitors resist sudden voltage changes; inductors resist sudden current changes — a useful symmetry for remembering both components together.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why does a fully-charged capacitor behave like an open circuit to steady DC, but still seem to "pass" an AC signal?

Once charged to the applied voltage, no more charge needs to flow, so current drops to zero for steady DC. But an AC signal is constantly changing voltage, which means the capacitor is continuously charging and discharging in response -- this repeated charge/discharge current looks like the signal is passing through, even though no charge actually crosses the insulating gap between the plates.
Question 2

Why can suddenly interrupting current through an inductor (e.g. opening a switch) produce a dangerous voltage spike?

An inductor's voltage is V = L(dI/dt) -- it depends on how fast current changes, not the current's value. A sudden interruption makes dI/dt extremely large for a brief instant, which can drive V to a very high spike. This is why inductive loads like motors and relay coils are typically paired with a flyback diode to safely dissipate this energy.
Question 3

Why does increasing resistance R INCREASE the RC time constant but DECREASE the RL time constant?

In an RC circuit, larger R limits the charging current available to the capacitor, slowing the process, so τ=RC increases with R. In an RL circuit, larger R limits the final steady-state current (by Ohm's Law) and the circuit reaches that smaller target faster, so τ=L/R decreases as R increases -- the two circuits' time constants respond to R in opposite directions.

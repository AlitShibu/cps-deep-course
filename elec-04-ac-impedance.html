AC Circuits & Impedance

So far every signal has been DC — constant over time. Real signals are very often AC (alternating current): sensor outputs, audio, radio, and mains power all vary continuously. This chapter extends Ohm's Law to handle time-varying sinusoidal signals, introducing impedance — the AC generalization of resistance — and uses it to explain how simple RC/RL circuits from Chapter 3 double as frequency-selective filters.

Section 4.1

Sinusoidal signals

An AC signal's voltage oscillates over time, most commonly as a sine wave — the natural shape produced by rotating machinery (generators) and the standard waveform used to analyze AC circuits generally, since more complex periodic signals can be built from sums of sine waves (a fact you'll meet formally later as the Fourier series).

v(t) = V_peak × sin(2π × f × t + φ)
  V_peak = the maximum voltage the signal reaches
  f = frequency (hertz, Hz) -- oscillations per second
  t = time (seconds)
  φ (phi) = phase offset (radians) -- shifts the wave left/right in time
One full cycle takes a period T = 1/f seconds; the wave repeats indefinitely.

Peak, peak-to-peak, and RMS values

A sine wave's voltage is constantly changing, so "the voltage" needs clarification about which specific value is meant:

V_peak       = the maximum value reached (from 0 to the top of the wave)
V_pk-pk      = from the most negative point to the most positive point = 2 × V_peak
V_RMS        = V_peak / √2 ≈ 0.707 × V_peak    ("root mean square")
Why RMS is the value that actually matters for power

RMS voltage is defined as the DC voltage that would deliver the exact same average power to a resistive load as the AC signal does. Since an AC voltage spends time at every value between 0 and V_peak, its instantaneous power (P=V²/R) varies constantly too — RMS is the one number that lets you use ordinary DC power formulas (P=V²/R) directly with an AC signal and get the correct average power. This is why household mains voltage (e.g. "230V AC") is always quoted as an RMS value, not a peak value.

Section 4.2

Impedance of R, L, and C

Impedance (symbol Z, measured in ohms) is the AC generalization of resistance — the total opposition a component presents to an AC signal, which for capacitors and inductors depends on frequency, unlike a resistor's fixed value.

Z_R = R                       (a resistor's impedance doesn't depend on frequency)
Z_L = 2π × f × L = ωL          (an inductor's impedance INCREASES with frequency)
Z_C = 1 / (2π × f × C) = 1/(ωC)  (a capacitor's impedance DECREASES with frequency)

  ω (omega) = 2πf, "angular frequency" (radians/second) -- a common shorthand
Why inductor impedance rises and capacitor impedance falls with frequency

An inductor opposes changes in current (Chapter 3) — a higher-frequency signal changes direction more often per second, so the inductor fights it harder, meaning more opposition (higher impedance) at higher frequency. A capacitor, by contrast, only "blocks" a truly constant (0 Hz) signal; the faster a signal alternates, the less time the capacitor spends anywhere near fully charged in either direction, so it presents progressively less opposition — lower impedance — as frequency increases. At DC (f=0): Z_L=0 (a plain wire) and Z_C=infinite (an open circuit) — exactly matching the fully-DC behavior described in Chapter 3.

Ohm's Law still works — with impedance in place of resistance

V = I × Z is the AC form of Ohm's Law, and it works the same way series/parallel combination rules do in Chapter 2 — impedances combine the same way resistances do (though the full arithmetic technically requires complex numbers, briefly introduced next, since impedance also carries a phase relationship between voltage and current).

Section 4.3

A brief look at phasors and complex impedance

In a resistor, voltage and current rise and fall perfectly in step (in phase). In a capacitor or inductor, they don't — current and voltage are shifted in time relative to each other, described by a phase angle. Fully tracking both magnitude and phase together is done using complex numbers, where impedance is written as a complex quantity: Z = R + jX (R is resistance, X is "reactance," j is the imaginary unit).

Why capacitors and inductors shift phase in opposite directions

In a capacitor, current has to flow before voltage can build up across it (charge must accumulate first) — current leads voltage. In an inductor, voltage appears in response to a changing current, so voltage leads current. This is memorable through the classic phrase "ELI the ICE man": in an inductor (L), voltage (E) leads current (I) — "ELI"; in a capacitor (C), current (I) leads voltage (E) — "ICE."

This course treats the complex-number machinery only at the conceptual level — the key takeaway for now is that AC circuit analysis genuinely does need to track a phase relationship, not just a magnitude, and that this is precisely what phasors and complex impedance are built to handle. A dedicated signals/circuits course will develop this formally with full complex arithmetic.

Section 4.4

Basic filters: low-pass and high-pass

Because a capacitor's impedance depends on frequency, an RC voltage divider (Chapter 2's concept, applied here with impedance instead of plain resistance) passes some frequencies more than others — this is a filter.

Low-pass filter (R in series, C to ground, output taken across C)

At low frequencies, Z_C is large — most of the input voltage divider's share goes to the capacitor, so the output (taken across C) is close to the full input. At high frequencies, Z_C shrinks toward zero — the output drops toward zero. Low frequencies pass through relatively unattenuated; high frequencies are attenuated. Hence, low-pass.

High-pass filter (C in series, R to ground, output taken across R)

Swap the roles: at low frequencies Z_C is large, so most voltage drops across the capacitor rather than the resistor — the output (across R) is small. At high frequencies Z_C shrinks, so more voltage appears across R — the output rises. High frequencies pass through; low frequencies are attenuated. Hence, high-pass.

f_cutoff = 1 / (2π × R × C)
The cutoff frequency is where the two impedances are equal

At the cutoff frequency, Z_C = R exactly — the voltage divider splits evenly, roughly halving the signal's power at that point (about 70.7% of the amplitude, "the −3dB point" in signal-processing terminology). Below cutoff, a low-pass filter's output tracks the input closely; above cutoff, it falls off. The exact same RC values from Chapter 3's timing circuit, viewed through a frequency lens instead of a time lens, directly determine this cutoff point — time-domain and frequency-domain behavior are two views of the exact same physical circuit.

Section 4.5

Worked problem — designing a low-pass filter

Problem

You need a low-pass filter to remove high-frequency electrical noise above 1kHz from a sensor signal, while passing frequencies below that mostly untouched. Using a standard 10nF capacitor, what resistor value gives a cutoff frequency of approximately 1kHz?

# Rearranging f_cutoff = 1 / (2πRC) to solve for R:
R = 1 / (2π × f_cutoff × C)
R = 1 / (2π × 1000 × 0.00000001)
R = 1 / (6.28 × 10⁻⁵)
R ≈ 15,900 Ω ≈ 16 kΩ

Why this makes sense dimensionally: a smaller capacitor requires a larger resistor to reach the same cutoff frequency (their product RC must stay constant for a fixed cutoff) — this is the same τ=RC relationship from Chapter 3, just being used to hit a target frequency response instead of a target time delay. A 16kΩ resistor paired with this 10nF capacitor gives exactly the frequency-domain behavior calculated here, and the same time-domain charging behavior (τ≈160µs) described in Section 3.2.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. AC signals are usually sinusoidal: v(t)=V_peak·sin(2πft+φ). RMS voltage (V_peak/√2) is the value that plugs directly into DC power formulas to give correct average AC power.
  2. Impedance (Z) generalizes resistance for AC: Z_R=R (constant), Z_L=ωL (rises with frequency), Z_C=1/(ωC) (falls with frequency) — matching each component's known DC-limit behavior from Chapter 3.
  3. Capacitors and inductors introduce a phase shift between voltage and current — remembered via "ELI the ICE man": inductor voltage leads current, capacitor current leads voltage.
  4. An RC voltage divider acts as a filter: output across C gives low-pass, output across R gives high-pass. The cutoff frequency f=1/(2πRC) is where Z_C=R.
  5. The same RC values determine both a circuit's time-domain response (τ=RC, Chapter 3) and its frequency-domain response (f_cutoff=1/2πRC) — two views of one physical circuit.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why is RMS voltage, not peak voltage, the value used in standard power formulas for AC signals?

RMS voltage is defined as the DC voltage that would deliver the same average power to a resistive load as the actual AC signal. Because AC voltage constantly varies, using peak voltage directly in P=V²/R would overstate the actual average power delivered; RMS is specifically constructed so DC power formulas give the correct average AC power.
Question 2

Why does a capacitor's impedance decrease as frequency increases, while an inductor's impedance increases?

A capacitor only fully blocks a truly constant signal; at higher frequencies it spends less time near full charge in either direction, presenting less opposition. An inductor opposes CHANGES in current, and a higher-frequency signal changes direction more often per second, so the inductor fights it more, presenting more opposition. This matches their DC limits: Z_C is infinite at 0 Hz, Z_L is zero at 0 Hz.
Question 3

In an RC low-pass filter (output taken across the capacitor), why does the output drop at high frequencies?

At high frequencies, the capacitor's impedance Z_C becomes very small compared to R. In the voltage divider formed by R and C, most of the input voltage now drops across R instead, leaving very little across C -- and since the output is taken across C, it shrinks toward zero at high frequencies.

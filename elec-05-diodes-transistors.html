Diodes & Transistors

Every component up to this point (resistor, capacitor, inductor) is linear and passive — its behavior is symmetric and doesn't depend on the direction of current, and it can't amplify a signal. Diodes and transistors break both rules, and that's exactly what makes them powerful: diodes enforce a one-way direction on current, and transistors act as electrically-controlled switches or amplifiers. These are the building blocks inside every digital logic gate (Digital Logic Chapter 2) and every microcontroller pin driver you'll work with in embedded projects.

Section 5.1

Semiconductor basics: the P-N junction

Silicon, in its pure form, is a poor conductor. Doping it with trace impurities creates two useful variants: N-type silicon (doped to have extra free electrons — negative charge carriers) and P-type silicon (doped to have "holes," an absence of an electron that behaves like a mobile positive charge carrier). Neither variant alone is especially interesting — the useful behavior appears at the boundary where the two types meet, called a P-N junction.

Why a P-N junction only conducts in one direction

At the junction, free electrons from the N-side and holes from the P-side diffuse across and combine, creating a thin "depletion region" with no free charge carriers, which acts as a barrier. Applying voltage in one direction (forward bias: P-side positive relative to N-side) pushes carriers toward the junction, narrowing the depletion region and eventually allowing current to flow. Applying voltage the other way (reverse bias) pulls carriers away from the junction, widening the depletion region and blocking current almost entirely. This asymmetric barrier is the entire physical basis for a diode's one-way behavior.

Section 5.2

Diodes: behavior and applications

A diode is a single P-N junction packaged as a two-terminal component, marked with a band on the physical device indicating the cathode (N-side); the other terminal is the anode (P-side). Current flows easily from anode to cathode (forward) and is blocked in the reverse direction.

Forward bias (anode +, cathode −):
  - Below ~0.7V (silicon diode): negligible current flows
  - Above ~0.7V: current rises very steeply -- the diode "turns on"
  - This ~0.7V is the diode's FORWARD VOLTAGE DROP, and it stays
    roughly constant across a wide range of currents once conducting

Reverse bias (anode −, cathode +):
  - Only a tiny leakage current flows (often treated as ~0 in basic analysis)
  - Beyond a component-specific REVERSE BREAKDOWN VOLTAGE, current
    suddenly increases sharply -- normal diodes are not meant to operate here
The ~0.7V forward drop is exactly what Chapter 1's LED problem used

An LED is a specialized type of diode (Light-Emitting Diode), and Chapter 1's worked problem used a 2V forward drop specifically because LEDs (built from different semiconductor materials than ordinary silicon diodes) have a higher, color-dependent forward voltage — commonly ~1.8-2.2V for red/amber LEDs and higher for blue/white. A standard silicon signal diode's ~0.7V drop is the more typical figure you'll see quoted for general-purpose rectifier and switching diodes.

Rectification: converting AC to DC

Because a diode only conducts in one direction, placing one in series with an AC source blocks the negative half of each cycle, leaving only the positive half — a half-wave rectifier. A full-wave bridge rectifier (four diodes arranged so both halves of the AC cycle get redirected the same way) is the standard building block inside virtually every DC power supply that plugs into AC mains, covered further in Chapter 7.

Why a flyback diode protects against inductive spikes (Chapter 3, revisited)

Chapter 3 flagged that suddenly interrupting current through an inductor (a motor or relay coil) causes a dangerous voltage spike. A diode placed in reverse-bias parallel across the inductive load (conducting nothing during normal operation) turns on the instant that spike tries to drive current the "wrong" way, giving the inductor's stored energy (½LI²) a safe path to dissipate instead of arcing across a switch or damaging a transistor driving the load. This is one of the most common practical diode applications in embedded hardware.

Section 5.3

Bipolar Junction Transistors (BJTs)

A BJT has three terminals — base (B), collector (C), and emitter (E) — and can be thought of as a P-N junction diode (base-emitter) that controls a much larger current flow (collector-emitter), using a small base current as the control signal.

I_C = β × I_B
  I_C = collector current (the "large" controlled current)
  I_B = base current (the small "control" current)
  β (beta) = current gain, typically 50-300 depending on the specific transistor
A transistor as a switch: using a small signal to control a large load

A microcontroller pin can typically only supply a few milliamps safely — nowhere near enough to drive a motor or a bright LED strip directly. Using that small pin current as I_B, a BJT can switch a collector current dozens to hundreds of times larger, letting a microcontroller safely control loads far beyond its own current capability. This is the single most common reason a hobbyist or embedded circuit includes a transistor at all — as a controlled switch, not necessarily as an amplifier.

A transistor switch still needs a base resistor

The base-emitter junction is itself a diode — connecting it directly to a voltage source without a current-limiting resistor (exactly like Chapter 1's LED problem) risks driving excessive base current and destroying the transistor. A base resistor, sized the same way as the LED problem (accounting for the ~0.7V base-emitter drop), is standard practice any time a transistor is switched from a digital output pin.

Saturation and cutoff: the two switch states

In cutoff (I_B ≈ 0), the transistor is fully off — no collector current flows, behaving like an open switch. In saturation (I_B large enough that further increases don't increase I_C proportionally), the transistor is fully on, behaving like a closed switch with a small residual voltage drop. Digital switching applications deliberately drive the transistor into one of these two extremes, avoiding the in-between "active" region (which is instead exploited for analog amplification, briefly noted below).

Section 5.4

MOSFETs — the modern default switch

A MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) also has three terminals — gate (G), drain (D), source (S) — but is controlled by voltage at the gate rather than current, since the gate is electrically insulated from the rest of the device and draws essentially zero steady current.

Why MOSFETs are the more common choice in modern embedded designs

Because the gate draws virtually no current, a MOSFET can be switched directly and efficiently by a microcontroller's digital output pin (which is itself a voltage source) without wasting current on a base resistor's continuous draw, and without a BJT's inherent β-dependent behavior varying between individual transistors. This is why MOSFETs dominate modern power-switching applications (motor drivers, LED drivers, DC-DC converters) even though BJTs remain common in simpler, lower-power switching and in classic analog amplifier designs.

For an N-channel MOSFET used as a low-side switch:
  V_GS below threshold (~1-4V, varies by part)  -> OFF, no current flows drain to source
  V_GS above threshold                             -> ON, current flows drain to source
                                                        (resistance in this "on" state,
                                                         R_DS(on), should be as low as possible)
Not every MOSFET turns fully on at 3.3V or 5V logic levels

Some MOSFETs are designed for higher gate-drive voltages (10V+) to reach their fully-on, low-resistance state — driving them from a microcontroller's 3.3V or 5V logic pin may leave them only partially on, causing excess heat from a higher-than-expected R_DS(on). Datasheets specifically list "logic-level" MOSFETs as suitable for direct microcontroller-pin control; checking this specification before selecting a part for a microcontroller-driven switching circuit is a genuinely common practical gotcha.

Section 5.5

Worked problem — sizing a BJT base resistor to drive a relay

Problem

A microcontroller pin outputs 5V and needs to switch on a relay coil drawing 100mA through the collector. The transistor has β=100. What base resistor value ensures the transistor saturates reliably?

# Step 1: Find the MINIMUM base current needed, then over-drive for reliable saturation
I_B(minimum) = I_C / β = 0.1A / 100 = 1 mA

# Standard practice: drive at least 2-5x this minimum to guarantee full saturation
# across manufacturing variation in β -- use a design target of I_B = 3 mA

# Step 2: Apply Ohm's Law to the base circuit, accounting for the ~0.7V base-emitter drop
# (exactly the LED-resistor pattern from Chapter 1's worked problem)
R_base = (V_pin - V_BE) / I_B = (5V - 0.7V) / 0.003A = 4.3V / 0.003A ≈ 1433 Ω

# A standard 1.2kΩ resistor (a common available value close to this) is the practical choice

Why over-driving the base current is standard practice, not sloppiness: β varies significantly between individual transistors of the same part number, and also with temperature — designing for exactly the calculated minimum risks the transistor not fully saturating under real-world variation, leaving unwanted resistance (and heat) in the collector-emitter path. Deliberately supplying several times the calculated minimum base current is a standard, deliberate margin, not an error.

Why this problem doesn't forget the flyback diode: a relay coil is exactly the inductive load described in Section 5.2 — a real design here would also include a flyback diode across the relay coil to protect the transistor from the coil's collapsing-field voltage spike when it switches off.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. A P-N junction conducts easily in forward bias and blocks current in reverse bias, due to an asymmetric depletion region — this is the physical basis of a diode.
  2. A diode has a roughly constant forward voltage drop (~0.7V silicon, ~2V+ for LEDs) once conducting. Rectifiers use diodes to convert AC to DC; a flyback diode protects switching transistors from inductive voltage spikes.
  3. A BJT uses a small base current to control a much larger collector current (I_C=βI_B) — commonly used as a current-controlled switch, and needs a base resistor to avoid destroying the base-emitter junction.
  4. A MOSFET is controlled by gate voltage rather than current, drawing essentially no steady gate current — the modern default for microcontroller-driven power switching, provided a "logic-level" part is used at 3.3V/5V drive.
  5. Digital switching applications drive a transistor fully into cutoff (off) or saturation (on), avoiding the linear region used for analog amplification (previewed further in Chapter 6).
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Physically, why does a P-N junction block current in reverse bias but conduct in forward bias?

Forward bias pushes charge carriers toward the junction, narrowing the depletion region until current can flow. Reverse bias pulls carriers away from the junction, widening the depletion region and blocking current. This asymmetric barrier behavior is the physical basis of a diode's one-way conduction.
Question 2

Why does a relay coil or motor need a flyback diode when switched by a transistor?

An inductive load (like a relay coil) resists sudden changes in current. When the transistor switches off and interrupts that current abruptly, the collapsing magnetic field can produce a very high voltage spike (V=L·dI/dt) that could damage the transistor. A flyback diode, wired in reverse across the coil, gives that stored energy a safe path to dissipate instead.
Question 3

Why are MOSFETs generally preferred over BJTs for microcontroller-driven power switching?

A MOSFET's gate is voltage-controlled and draws essentially no steady current, so a microcontroller pin can drive it directly and efficiently without a continuously-drawing base resistor, and without depending on a BJT's variable current gain (β). This makes MOSFETs more efficient and more predictable for power-switching applications, provided a logic-level part suited to the microcontroller's voltage is chosen.

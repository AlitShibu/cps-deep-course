Vectors

Before matrices, before eigenvalues, before any of the machinery that makes control theory and robotics work — you need one solid, multi-angle understanding of what a vector actually is, and complete fluency with the handful of operations you can do to one. Everything else in this course sits on top of this chapter.

Section 1.1

What a vector actually is — three views of the same thing

Most beginners meet vectors through exactly one lens (usually "an arrow" or "a list of numbers") and then get confused later when a different course presents it completely differently. It's the same object every time — you're better off holding all three views at once from day one.

View 1: A vector is a quantity with both size and direction

This is the physics-flavored definition. "The car is moving at 60 km/h" is missing information — 60 km/h which way? "60 km/h, heading north-east" is a vector quantity: it has a magnitude (60 km/h) and a direction (north-east). Compare this to a scalar — a quantity with size but no direction, like temperature or mass: "25°C" is complete on its own; it doesn't need a direction to mean something.

View 2: A vector is an arrow

Geometrically, a vector is drawn as an arrow: the length of the arrow represents magnitude, and the way it points represents direction. Crucially, a vector's identity doesn't depend on where you draw it — an arrow 3 units long pointing due east is "the same vector" whether you draw its tail at the origin or three miles away. What matters is length and direction, not position. This is different from a point, which is exactly about position.

A vector is defined by length + direction only — not by its starting point.

View 3: A vector is an ordered list of numbers

This is the view you'll actually compute with. In 2D, a vector is written as a pair of numbers — its components along the x and y axes. The vector from the origin to the point (3, 4) is written:

v = (3, 4)      or as a column:      v = [3]
                                          [4]

These numbers tell you exactly how far to move along each axis to trace the arrow: 3 units right, 4 units up. This is the view that generalizes cleanly to any number of dimensions — a vector in 3D has 3 components, and in CPS work you'll routinely deal with vectors in far more than 3 dimensions (a robot's full state — position, velocity, joint angles — might be a 12-dimensional vector), where "draw an arrow" stops being possible to visualize but the number-list definition works identically.

Reconciling all three views

The magnitude+direction view and the arrow view are the same idea in different words — "arrow" is just the picture of "magnitude and direction." The number-list view is what lets you actually compute with that arrow: given components (3, 4), you can calculate the arrow's exact length and angle (coming up in Section 1.4), and every operation you'll learn in this chapter has both a geometric meaning (what happens to the arrow) and an algebraic recipe (what happens to the numbers). Good vector intuition means being able to translate between these two freely, in both directions.

Notation you'll see across textbooks and code

NotationWhere you'll see it
v (bold) or v⃗ (arrow on top)Textbooks — distinguishes a vector from a plain scalar variable.
(3, 4)Row form — common when writing vectors inline in text.
[3, 4]ᵀ or a vertical columnColumn form — the "T" (transpose) means "written as a row here, but treat it as a column." Matters once matrix multiplication (Chapter 2) cares about row-vs-column shape.

This course will mostly write vectors as (x, y) or (x, y, z) in prose, and as NumPy arrays in code — matching how you'll actually encounter them day to day.

Section 1.2

Vector addition and subtraction

Addition — geometric picture: tip-to-tail

To add two vectors u and v geometrically, place the tail of v at the tip (arrowhead) of u. The sum u + v is the single arrow from the start of u to the end of v. This is called the tip-to-tail rule, and it directly models physical situations: if you walk along displacement u, then along displacement v, your total displacement is u + v — regardless of the two individual paths.

Place v's tail at u's tip; the sum is the direct arrow from start to finish.

Addition — algebraic recipe: add components

Numerically, vector addition is simply adding matching components:

u = (u₁, u₂)
v = (v₁, v₂)
u + v = (u₁ + v₁, u₂ + v₂)

Example: (3, 4) + (1, -2) = (4, 2). This generalizes to any number of dimensions — add the first components together, the second components together, and so on.

Subtraction — what u − v really means

Subtraction is addition of a negated vector: u − v = u + (−v), where −v is v flipped 180° (same length, opposite direction). Geometrically and usefully: u − v is the vector that points from the tip of v to the tip of u (when both are drawn from the same starting point) — this is exactly how you compute a relative displacement, e.g. "where is the target relative to me," which comes up constantly in robotics and vehicle dynamics.

u - v = (u₁ - v₁, u₂ - v₂)
Worked problem

Problem: A drone is at position A = (2, 5) meters. Its target is at B = (10, 1) meters. Find the displacement vector from the drone to the target, and its individual axis components.

Solution: The displacement from A to B is B − A (tip minus tail, target minus current position):

displacement = B - A = (10 - 2, 1 - 5) = (8, -4)

Reading the result: the drone needs to move 8 meters in the +x direction and 4 meters in the −y direction to reach the target. Notice the order matters — A − B would give (−8, 4), the same displacement but backward (target-to-drone instead of drone-to-target). Getting this order backward is one of the most common early mistakes, so it's worth saying explicitly: "displacement from A to B" is always B − A, endpoint minus start.

In NumPy

import numpy as np

A = np.array([2, 5])
B = np.array([10, 1])

displacement = B - A
print(displacement)   # [ 8 -4]

NumPy overloads + and - to do exactly this component-wise arithmetic automatically — you never write a loop over components by hand.

Section 1.3

Scalar multiplication

The geometric picture: stretching, shrinking, flipping

Multiplying a vector by a plain number (a scalar) changes its length without changing the line it lies on — unless the scalar is negative, in which case it also flips the direction 180°. Multiply by 2, the arrow doubles in length. Multiply by 0.5, it shrinks to half. Multiply by −1, it points the exact opposite way, same length.

Scalar multiplication only ever stretches, shrinks, or flips a vector — it never changes the line it lies on.

The algebraic recipe

Multiply every component by the scalar:

k * v = k * (v₁, v₂) = (k*v₁, k*v₂)

Example: 3 * (2, -1) = (6, -3).

Linear combinations — addition and scaling together

Combining scalar multiplication with addition gives you a linear combination — arguably the single most important operation in all of linear algebra, because Chapters 2 and 5 are almost entirely built on this idea:

result = a*u + b*v

where a and b are scalars and u, v are vectors. This says: "stretch/shrink/flip u by a, stretch/shrink/flip v by b, then add the results." Every vector you can reach by choosing different values of a and b (with fixed u and v) is said to be in the span of u and v — a concept Chapter 5 builds on directly.

Worked problem

Problem: A robot's target velocity is built from two independent thruster directions: u = (1, 0) (forward) and v = (0, 1) (sideways). If the controller commands 3 units of forward thrust and −2 units of sideways thrust, what's the resulting velocity vector?

Solution: This is exactly the linear combination 3u + (-2)v:

3*(1,0) + (-2)*(0,1) = (3,0) + (0,-2) = (3,-2)

The robot's actual velocity is (3, -2) — 3 units forward, 2 units in the negative sideways direction. This exact pattern — combining independent "basis" directions with scalar weights — is precisely how a drone's four motors combine to produce a single net thrust direction, and it's the seed of the "basis" concept formalized in Chapter 5.

In NumPy

import numpy as np
u = np.array([1, 0])
v = np.array([0, 1])
result = 3*u + (-2)*v
print(result)   # [ 3 -2]
Section 1.4

Magnitude (norm) and unit vectors

Magnitude — how "long" a vector is

The magnitude (also called the norm, written |v| or ‖v‖) of a vector is its length as an arrow — always a non-negative scalar. For a 2D vector, this comes directly from the Pythagorean theorem: the vector's components form the two legs of a right triangle, and the magnitude is the hypotenuse.

The magnitude is the hypotenuse of the right triangle formed by a vector's components.
|v| = √(v₁² + v₂²)          (2D)
|v| = √(v₁² + v₂² + v₃²)    (3D)
|v| = √(v₁² + v₂² + ... + vₙ²)   (n dimensions — same pattern, however many components)
Worked problem

Problem: Find the magnitude of v = (3, 4), and of w = (1, 2, 2).

Solution:

|v| = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5
|w| = √(1² + 2² + 2²) = √(1 + 4 + 4) = √9 = 3

The (3,4,5) result isn't a coincidence to memorize — it's the same 3-4-5 right triangle from basic geometry, just relabeled as a vector.

Unit vectors — direction with the length stripped out

A unit vector has magnitude exactly 1. Any non-zero vector can be converted into a unit vector pointing the same direction by dividing every component by the vector's own magnitude — this process is called normalizing a vector:

v̂ = v / |v|

The "hat" symbol (v̂) is the standard notation for "this is a unit vector." Normalizing is one of the most common operations in CPS code — whenever you care about direction only and want to discard magnitude (a heading direction for a vehicle, a surface normal in computer vision, a control direction in a PID output), you normalize first.

Why this matters

Normalizing lets you separate "which way" from "how much" cleanly. For example, if you have a robot's velocity vector and want to know its heading independent of speed, you normalize the velocity vector — the result tells you the direction, and you can pair it with the original magnitude (the speed) separately whenever you need both pieces of information again.

Pitfall

Normalizing the zero vector (0, 0) means dividing by |v| = 0 — undefined, and your code will either crash or silently produce NaN (Not-a-Number) values. Any normalization function you write for real use should check for a zero (or near-zero, due to floating-point noise) magnitude first and handle it explicitly — usually by returning a zero vector or raising an error, depending on what makes sense for your application.

In NumPy

import numpy as np

v = np.array([3, 4])
magnitude = np.linalg.norm(v)      # 5.0
unit_v = v / magnitude              # [0.6, 0.8]
print(np.linalg.norm(unit_v))      # 1.0 — confirms it's a true unit vector

# Safe normalize, handling the zero-vector case
def normalize(v, eps=1e-10):
    mag = np.linalg.norm(v)
    if mag < eps:
        return np.zeros_like(v)
    return v / mag
Section 1.5

The dot product

If this chapter has one operation you need to walk away fluent in, it's this one — the dot product resurfaces in eigenvalues (Ch. 6), projections (this section), control theory, sensor fusion, and computer vision. It has two definitions that look unrelated at first and are secretly the same fact.

Definition 1 — the algebraic recipe

Multiply matching components, then add everything up:

u · v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ + ... + uₙvₙ

Example: (2, 3) · (4, -1) = 2×4 + 3×(-1) = 8 - 3 = 5. Note the result is a single scalar number, not a vector — this is why it's sometimes called the "scalar product."

Definition 2 — the geometric meaning

u · v = |u| |v| cos(θ)

where θ is the angle between the two vectors. This says the dot product measures how aligned two vectors are, scaled by their lengths: maximal when they point the same way (θ=0°, cos=1), zero when perpendicular (θ=90°, cos=0), and negative when pointing more away from each other than toward (θ>90°).

The dot product's sign and size tell you how aligned two vectors are — a single number capturing a geometric relationship.
Why the two definitions are the same fact (the derivation)

This isn't a coincidence — it follows directly from the law of cosines, the generalization of the Pythagorean theorem to non-right triangles: for a triangle with sides a, b, c and angle θ opposite side c, c² = a² + b² − 2ab·cos(θ). Draw u, v, and the vector u − v connecting their tips as a triangle, apply the law of cosines with |u|, |v|, and |u−v| as the three sides, then expand |u−v|² using components — the algebra collapses to exactly u·v = |u||v|cos(θ). You don't need to reproduce this derivation from memory, but knowing it exists is reassuring: the two definitions aren't a lucky pattern, they're mathematically forced to agree.

The most common practical use: testing for perpendicularity

Since cos(90°) = 0, two non-zero vectors are exactly perpendicular (orthogonal) if and only if their dot product is zero. This is by far the most common practical use of the dot product in code — a single multiply-and-add check replaces any need to actually compute an angle:

def are_perpendicular(u, v, tol=1e-9):
    return abs(np.dot(u, v)) < tol

Finding the angle between two vectors

Rearranging Definition 2 gives you the angle directly:

θ = arccos( (u·v) / (|u| |v|) )
Worked problem

Problem: Two force vectors act on a bracket: F1 = (3, 4) and F2 = (4, -3) (units of N). Are they perpendicular? If not, find the angle between them.

Solution: Compute the dot product first: F1 · F2 = 3×4 + 4×(-3) = 12 - 12 = 0. Since the dot product is exactly zero, the two forces are perpendicular — no need to even compute the angle separately, since we already know it's 90°. (As a sanity check using magnitudes: |F1| = 5, |F2| = 5, so cos(θ) = 0/25 = 0, giving θ = 90° — consistent.)

Vector projection — decomposing one vector along another

A closely related, extremely useful operation: the projection of u onto v answers "how much of u points in the v direction?" — it's the "shadow" u would cast onto the line that v lies on, if light shone perpendicular to v.

proj_v(u) = ( (u·v) / |v|² ) * v
Projection strips out only the part of u that lies along v's direction.
Worked problem

Problem: A wind force F = (5, 5) N acts on a sailboat moving in direction d = (10, 0) (due east). How much of the wind force actually contributes to forward motion (i.e., project F onto d)?

Solution:

F·d = 5×10 + 5×0 = 50
|d|² = 10² + 0² = 100
proj_d(F) = (50/100) * (10, 0) = 0.5 * (10, 0) = (5, 0)

Only the (5, 0) component of the wind force actually pushes the boat forward — the other (0, 5) component (perpendicular to the direction of travel) contributes nothing to forward motion, and in a real sailing scenario would instead push the boat sideways, resisted by the keel.

In NumPy

import numpy as np

def project(u, v):
    return (np.dot(u, v) / np.dot(v, v)) * v

F = np.array([5, 5])
d = np.array([10, 0])
print(project(F, d))   # [5. 0.]
Section 1.6

The cross product (3D)

Unlike the dot product (which works in any number of dimensions and returns a scalar), the cross product is specifically a 3D operation and returns another vector — one perpendicular to both inputs. It shows up heavily in robotics (torque, angular velocity, surface normals) which is why it earns a place here even though it's used less constantly than the dot product.

The geometric meaning

u × v produces a vector that is perpendicular to both u and v simultaneously, with a direction given by the right-hand rule (point your right hand's fingers along u, curl them toward v, your thumb points along u × v), and a magnitude equal to the area of the parallelogram that u and v span:

|u × v| = |u| |v| sin(θ)
The cross product's direction is perpendicular to the plane containing u and v; its length equals the enclosed parallelogram's area.

The algebraic recipe (determinant formula)

u × v = ( u₂v₃ - u₃v₂,  u₃v₁ - u₁v₃,  u₁v₂ - u₂v₁ )

This looks arbitrary memorized cold — it's easier to remember via the determinant pattern (Chapter 4 covers determinants properly, but the shape is worth seeing now):

       | i   j   k  |
u × v = | u₁  u₂  u₃ |
       | v₁  v₂  v₃ |

where i, j, k are the unit vectors along x, y, z. Expanding this determinant along the top row produces exactly the three-component formula above.

Worked problem

Problem: A wrench applies force F = (0, 10, 0) N at a position r = (0.3, 0, 0) m from a bolt's center (r is the "lever arm"). Find the resulting torque τ = r × F.

Solution: Using the formula with r = (0.3, 0, 0) and F = (0, 10, 0):

τ₁ = r₂F₃ - r₃F₂ = 0×0 - 0×10 = 0
τ₂ = r₃F₁ - r₁F₃ = 0×0 - 0.3×0 = 0
τ₃ = r₁F₂ - r₂F₁ = 0.3×10 - 0×0 = 3

τ = (0, 0, 3)   N·m

The torque points entirely along the z-axis, with magnitude 3 N·m — physically, this means the wrench produces a rotation purely around the z-axis (into/out of the page in a standard xy-plane view), with no rotational tendency around the x or y axes. This is exactly how a robotics or mechanics simulation computes rotational effects from applied forces.

In NumPy

import numpy as np
r = np.array([0.3, 0, 0])
F = np.array([0, 10, 0])
tau = np.cross(r, F)
print(tau)   # [0. 0. 3.]
Pitfall — dot vs. cross confusion

These are genuinely easy to mix up under pressure. A quick anchor: dot product → a number, measures alignment, works in any dimension. Cross product → a vector, measures "spread apart-ness" (perpendicularity), only defined in 3D. If your code produces a vector where you expected a single number (or vice versa), that's an immediate signal you've called the wrong one.

Section 1.7

Linear combinations and span — a first look

This section is deliberately brief here — it's the seed of Chapter 5's full treatment (vector spaces, basis, rank), but it belongs conceptually in this chapter because it's built from nothing but the two operations you now know: addition and scalar multiplication.

Given a set of vectors, a linear combination is any vector you can build by scaling each one and adding the results (Section 1.3 introduced this with two vectors — it generalizes to any number). The span of a set of vectors is the set of every vector reachable this way.

Why this matters, previewed

If two vectors point in genuinely different directions (neither is just a scaled copy of the other), their span covers the entire 2D plane — any point in the plane is reachable as some combination of the two. But if they happen to point along the same line, their span is only that one line, no matter how you scale them — you've lost a whole dimension of reachability. This exact idea — whether a set of vectors "loses a dimension" — is what Chapter 5 formalizes as linear independence and rank, and it's the mathematical reason a system of equations can fail to have a unique solution (Chapter 3) or a matrix can fail to have an inverse (Chapter 4). Filing this feeling away now will make those chapters click much faster.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. A vector has magnitude and direction; it can be viewed as an arrow (geometric) or an ordered list of components (algebraic) — the two views are always interchangeable.
  2. Addition is tip-to-tail geometrically, component-wise numerically. Subtraction u − v gives the displacement from v's tip to u's tip.
  3. Scalar multiplication stretches/shrinks/flips a vector without changing the line it lies on; combined with addition it forms a linear combination.
  4. Magnitude (norm) is the Pythagorean-theorem length of a vector; a unit vector (magnitude 1) captures pure direction, made by dividing a vector by its own magnitude.
  5. The dot product (u·v = Σuᵢvᵢ = |u||v|cos θ) measures alignment — zero exactly when vectors are perpendicular — and underlies both angle-finding and vector projection.
  6. The cross product (3D only) returns a vector perpendicular to both inputs, with magnitude equal to the spanned parallelogram's area — the basis of torque and angular quantities in mechanics.
  7. The set of every linear combination of a group of vectors is their span — whether that span "loses a dimension" is the seed of linear independence and rank, coming in Chapter 5.

Quick check

Before looking at the answers, try to answer each from memory — that's a far better test of whether this has actually stuck than re-reading the section.

Question 1

Given A = (1, 2) and B = (5, 8), what is the displacement vector from A to B, and what is its magnitude?

Displacement = B − A = (4, 6). Magnitude = √(4² + 6²) = √(16+36) = √52 ≈ 7.21.
Question 2

You compute u · v = 0 for two non-zero vectors. What does this tell you geometrically, and what would it mean instead if you'd computed u × v = (0,0,0)?

u · v = 0 means the vectors are perpendicular (90° apart). u × v = (0,0,0) means the opposite kind of special case — the vectors are parallel (pointing the same or exactly opposite direction), since the cross product's magnitude depends on sin(θ), which is zero at 0° and 180°.
Question 3

Why does normalizing a vector require a special check for the zero vector, and what typically goes wrong if you skip it?

Normalizing divides by the vector's own magnitude. The zero vector has magnitude 0, so this becomes division by zero — in code this typically produces NaN (Not-a-Number) values silently, rather than crashing loudly, which can let corrupted data propagate deep into a program before anyone notices.
Question 4

A force F = (6, 8) N acts along a direction d = (1, 0). What fraction of F's magnitude actually acts along d?

Project F onto d: proj = (F·d / d·d) * d = (6/1)*(1,0) = (6,0). So exactly 6 N of the force's 10 N magnitude (|F|=√(36+64)=10) acts along d — the other component (0,8) is entirely perpendicular to d and contributes nothing in that direction.

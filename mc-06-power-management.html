Power Management & Sleep Modes

A microcontroller plugged into mains power can run flat-out forever without a second thought. A microcontroller powered by a battery — the overwhelming majority of real embedded and IoT projects, including field-deployed sensor nodes — has a hard, finite energy budget, and power management becomes a first-class design concern rather than an afterthought. This chapter covers why, and how sleep modes let a battery-powered device last months or years instead of days.

Section 6.1

Why power management matters

Battery capacity is measured in milliamp-hours (mAh) — roughly, how many milliamps of current the battery can supply for how many hours before it's depleted. A 2000mAh battery powering a circuit that draws a constant 100mA lasts approximately 20 hours; the same battery powering a circuit averaging just 1mA lasts roughly 2000 hours (over 80 days) — a direct, dramatic illustration of why average current draw, not peak performance, determines real-world battery life.

This is Electronics Chapter 1's power equation, applied to a design decision

Battery life is fundamentally a units calculation: capacity (mAh) divided by average current draw (mA) gives hours of operation. An MCU running full-speed continuously might draw tens of milliamps; the same MCU in a deep sleep state can draw mere microamps — often a thousand-fold difference. Since most real sensor applications only need to actually do something occasionally (read a sensor once a minute, transmit data once an hour), the vast majority of a device's lifetime is spent doing genuinely nothing useful — which makes that idle time the single biggest lever available for extending battery life.

Section 6.2

Sleep modes: trading capability for power savings

MCUs offer multiple sleep modes, each disabling a different subset of internal hardware to save power — with a general trade-off: deeper sleep saves more power, but disables more capability and takes longer to wake from.

AVR (Arduino) sleep modes, from lightest to deepest:
  IDLE           -- CPU stops, most peripherals (timers, ADC) keep running
  ADC_NOISE_REDUCTION -- CPU + most peripherals stop, used for cleaner ADC readings
  POWER_SAVE / POWER_DOWN -- nearly everything stops except specific wake sources
                              (external interrupt, watchdog timer)

ESP32 sleep modes:
  Modem sleep    -- WiFi/Bluetooth radio off between active moments, CPU still runs
  Light sleep    -- CPU paused, RAM retained, wakes quickly
  Deep sleep     -- almost everything powered down, RAM NOT retained (except a
                     small dedicated "RTC memory" region) -- wakes essentially
                     by restarting the program from the top
Why deep sleep on the ESP32 loses your variables (and how RTC memory fixes it)

Deep sleep powers down the main SRAM entirely to minimize current draw — since SRAM is volatile (Chapter 1), everything in it, including your program's ordinary variables, is lost. A small separate RTC memory region stays powered even in deep sleep specifically to hold a handful of values across sleep cycles (a counter, a timestamp, calibration data) — variables intended to survive deep sleep must be explicitly declared to live in this special region, rather than as ordinary variables.

Deep sleep genuinely restarts your program from setup(), not from where it slept

Unlike lighter sleep modes that pause and resume execution mid-loop(), ESP32 deep sleep is effectively a full reset — the chip wakes up and runs setup() from the very beginning again, not from wherever loop() was when it went to sleep. Code that assumes execution simply "continues" after waking from deep sleep will behave completely unexpectedly; checking the wake-up reason (available via a dedicated API call) inside setup() is the standard way to distinguish "just powered on for the first time" from "woke up from deep sleep" and act accordingly.

Section 6.3

Waking up: timers and interrupts, again

A sleeping MCU needs a specific, pre-configured event to wake it back up — exactly the timer and interrupt mechanisms from Chapter 4, now used for a new purpose.

// ESP32 example: wake after a fixed time interval using the built-in timer wake source
#define SLEEP_SECONDS 60

void setup() {
    esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(SLEEP_SECONDS * 1000000ULL);  // microseconds
    // ... do sensor reading / transmission work here ...
    esp_deep_sleep_start();     // enter deep sleep -- execution stops here entirely
    // (code after this line never runs -- the chip restarts from setup() on wake)
}

void loop() {
    // often left empty -- all the work happens in setup() before sleeping again
}
Why this restructures the whole program around setup(), not loop()

Since deep sleep restarts execution from setup() every time, and the device typically wants to do its work, then sleep again, then repeat, the natural embedded pattern for a deep-sleep-based project moves nearly all the actual logic into setup() itself — loop() often does nothing at all, since the program never really "loops" in the traditional sense; each wake cycle is really a fresh, short-lived run of the whole program that ends in going back to sleep.

Besides a timer, an MCU can typically also wake from an external interrupt (a button press, a sensor's own interrupt output pin signaling new data is ready) — letting a device sleep indefinitely until something specific actually happens, rather than waking periodically to check nothing has changed.

Section 6.4

Practical current-draw considerations

Peripherals and sensors keep drawing current even while the MCU sleeps

Putting the MCU itself to sleep doesn't automatically turn off an attached sensor, display, or radio module — many of these draw meaningful current continuously unless explicitly powered down or put into their own low-power mode. A genuinely power-optimized design often switches an external sensor's power supply itself through a MOSFET (Electronics Chapter 5) controlled by a GPIO pin, cutting its current draw to zero during sleep rather than just leaving it powered and idle.

Voltage regulator inefficiency at very low currents — a subtle but real effect

A basic linear voltage regulator (Electronics Chapter 7) itself draws a small "quiescent current" just to operate, regardless of the load — at deep-sleep-level microamp currents, this regulator's own overhead can actually dominate total power consumption, sometimes drawing more current than the sleeping MCU itself. Battery-powered, sleep-optimized designs often specifically choose low-quiescent-current regulators (or bypass a regulator entirely when battery voltage is compatible directly with the MCU), precisely because the "supporting" circuitry can otherwise undo much of the sleep mode's power savings.

Section 6.5

Worked problem — estimating battery life for a deep-sleep sensor node

Problem

An ESP32 sensor node wakes every 60 seconds, takes a reading and transmits it (drawing 150mA for 2 seconds), then returns to deep sleep (drawing 15µA the rest of the time). Estimate battery life on a 2000mAh battery.

# Step 1: Average current during the "awake" portion
Awake:  150 mA for 2 seconds, out of every 60 seconds

# Step 2: Compute the TIME-WEIGHTED AVERAGE current over one full 60-second cycle
awake_fraction = 2/60 = 0.0333
sleep_fraction = 58/60 = 0.9667

avg_current = (0.0333 × 150mA) + (0.9667 × 0.015mA)
avg_current = 5.0 mA + 0.0145 mA
avg_current ≈ 5.01 mA

# Step 3: Estimate battery life
battery_life = capacity / avg_current = 2000 mAh / 5.01 mA ≈ 399 hours ≈ 16.6 days

Why the brief 150mA burst dominates the calculation despite being only 3.3% of the time: 150mA is roughly 10,000 times larger than the 15µA sleep current, so even a small time fraction spent at that high current contributes far more to the average than the overwhelming majority of time spent asleep — this is exactly why reducing either the transmission duration or its current draw (a more efficient radio protocol, a shorter data payload) often has a bigger impact on battery life than further optimizing an already-tiny sleep current.

Why this is only an estimate, not an exact real-world figure: real battery capacity isn't perfectly linear with discharge rate, self-discharge occurs over time regardless of load, and temperature affects both battery capacity and MCU current draw — this calculation gives a genuinely useful first-order estimate for comparing design choices (is 60-second or 5-minute wake interval better?), which is exactly the level of precision this kind of early design decision actually needs.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. Battery life = capacity (mAh) / average current draw (mA) — since most sensor applications are idle most of the time, minimizing idle current draw is usually the single biggest lever for extending battery life.
  2. Sleep modes trade capability for power savings — lighter modes (idle) keep most peripherals running; deeper modes (deep sleep) shut down almost everything, including (on ESP32) main SRAM itself.
  3. ESP32 deep sleep restarts execution from setup(), not from where loop() left off — variables that must survive across sleep cycles need to be explicitly placed in RTC memory.
  4. Waking from sleep uses the same timer/interrupt mechanisms from Chapter 4 — a fixed-interval timer wake, or an external interrupt to wake only when something specific actually happens.
  5. Attached peripherals (sensors, radios) keep drawing current unless explicitly powered down (often via a MOSFET switch); at very low sleep currents, even the voltage regulator's own quiescent current can become a significant, non-negligible factor.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why does ESP32 deep sleep lose ordinary variable values, and how can a value be preserved across a deep sleep cycle?

Deep sleep powers down the main SRAM to minimize current draw, and since SRAM is volatile, everything stored in it -- including ordinary variables -- is lost. A small separate RTC memory region stays powered even during deep sleep specifically to preserve a few values across sleep cycles; a variable must be explicitly declared to live in this special region to survive.
Question 2

In the battery-life worked problem, why does a brief 150mA transmission burst dominate the average current calculation even though it's only active 3.3% of the time?

150mA is roughly 10,000 times larger than the 15µA sleep current. Even though the high-current burst only occupies a small fraction of the total time, its contribution to the time-weighted average (fraction of time × current) vastly outweighs the contribution from the much longer but far smaller sleep current, so the brief burst ends up dominating the overall average.
Question 3

Why might putting the MCU into deep sleep fail to achieve the expected battery life savings in a real circuit?

Attached peripherals like sensors or radio modules often keep drawing current continuously unless they are also explicitly powered down or put into their own low-power state -- the MCU sleeping doesn't automatically turn them off. Additionally, at very low sleep-level currents, the voltage regulator's own quiescent current can become a significant fraction of total consumption, sometimes exceeding the sleeping MCU's own draw.

Serial Communication Fundamentals

Every sensor, display, and peripheral chip you will ever wire to a microcontroller talks over one of a small handful of serial protocols. Before naming any of them — UART, SPI, I2C, CAN — this chapter builds the vocabulary that all of them share: what a bit rate actually is, why we send data one wire at a time instead of many, how a receiver knows where one piece of data ends and the next begins, and the physical-layer facts (voltage levels, logic families, noise) that decide whether a wire actually carries a 1 or a 0 reliably. Everything in Chapters 2 through 5 is a specific set of answers to the questions this chapter raises.

Section 1.1

Serial vs. parallel communication

A computer's internal buses move many bits at once, side by side on separate wires — that is parallel communication. Serial communication sends the same bits one after another on a single wire (or a single differential pair). It sounds like a step backward, and for very short, very fast on-chip connections it often is. But the moment you leave the chip, serial wins for reasons that matter to every embedded design you will build.

ParallelSerial
Wires needed for N-bit dataN (plus ground, plus often a clock/strobe)1–4, regardless of how many bits total are sent
Cable cost and connector sizeGrows with word widthStays small and fixed
Skew (wires arriving at slightly different times)A real problem — all N bits must arrive togetherNone — only one bit is in flight at a time
Practical distance / speed at low costShort distance only (skew and crosstalk between wires get worse over length)Long distance is achievable (this is why USB, Ethernet, SATA, and PCIe are all serial today)
Why nearly everything moved to serial

Historically, PCs used wide parallel buses (IDE, the parallel printer port) because at short distance and moderate speed, more wires really did mean more throughput. As clock speeds rose, keeping N wires perfectly synchronized (no skew, no crosstalk) became the harder engineering problem, while a single fast serial line proved easier to drive reliably. USB, SATA, PCIe, and Ethernet all replaced older parallel standards for exactly this reason. In embedded systems the driving reasons are simpler and more concrete: pin count on a microcontroller is scarce and expensive, and a chip with 4 pins is far easier to route on a small PCB than one with 20.

Where each protocol in this topic sits

All four protocols covered in this course — UART, SPI, I2C, and CAN — are serial. They differ in how many wires they use, whether a shared clock is sent alongside the data, how many devices can share the same wires, and how far and how fast they can reliably go. Chapter 6 puts them side by side; every chapter until then builds up the vocabulary to make that comparison meaningful rather than a bare list of acronyms.

Section 1.2

Bit rate, baud rate, and the synchronous/asynchronous split

Bits do not send themselves at some abstract rate; a real clock, real or implied, decides when the receiver should look at the wire. This section defines the vocabulary you will see on every datasheet and every logic analyzer capture.

Bit rate and baud rate

Bit rate  = number of bits transmitted per second (bits per second, bps)
Baud rate = number of SYMBOL changes per second (baud)

For the simplest signaling — one wire, two voltage levels, one bit per symbol — bit rate and baud rate are numerically the same, which is why the terms get used interchangeably in casual conversation about UART. They stop being the same the moment a scheme sends more than one bit per symbol (for example, a signal with four distinguishable voltage levels can carry 2 bits per symbol, so its bit rate is twice its baud rate). This course deals almost entirely with the simple one-bit-per-symbol case, but knowing the distinction exists will save you from a real mix-up when you meet a datasheet for a modem, a radio module, or a high-speed serial standard that specifies baud and bit rate separately.

The bit period

If the bit rate is R bits/second, each bit occupies a bit period Tbit = 1/R seconds. A UART running at 9600 bps has Tbit = 1/9600 ≈ 104.2 µs per bit; at 115200 bps, Tbit ≈ 8.68 µs. This is not a side fact — it is the number you use to size a receiver's sampling timer, and it is the number that determines how far off a transmitter's and receiver's clocks are allowed to be before bits get misread (Chapter 2 makes this precise for UART).

A quick calculation you will do often

How long does it take to send one byte (with no framing overhead yet) at 1 Mbps? Tbit = 1/1,000,000 = 1 µs, and 8 bits takes 8 µs. Scale that up: transferring 1 KB (1024 bytes) of raw data takes roughly 1024 × 8 µs ≈ 8.2 ms — a number worth having a feel for when you decide whether a sensor read will finish inside a 1 ms control-loop tick (Control Systems Ch. 7's sampling-period discussion) or a 10 ms one.

Synchronous vs. asynchronous

The receiver must sample the wire at the same rate the transmitter is sending, or the bits blur together. There are exactly two strategies for making that happen:

SynchronousAsynchronous
How the receiver knows when to sampleA separate clock signal is sent alongside the data, and the receiver samples on every clock edgeNo shared clock wire; both sides agree on a rate in advance, and the receiver's own internal clock free-runs at that rate
Wires neededAt least one extra wire, for the clockNo extra clock wire
Timing toleranceEssentially perfect — the clock edge is the timing referenceLimited — the two independent clocks must agree closely enough not to drift by more than about half a bit period over one data frame
Examples in this courseSPI, I2C — the clock line is called SCLK or SCLUART — no clock wire at all
The trade-off in one sentence

Synchronous protocols trade an extra wire for perfect, drift-free timing; asynchronous protocols trade the extra wire away and accept a strict limit on how much the two clocks are allowed to disagree. UART's entire design (Chapter 2), from its start bit to its 1–2% baud-rate-accuracy requirement, exists to manage that trade-off without a shared clock.

Section 1.3

Framing: where does the data start and end?

A raw stream of bits is meaningless without agreed-upon boundaries: where does one piece of data end and the next begin? Framing is the set of rules that answers this, and every protocol in this course frames its data differently — but every framing scheme is solving the same underlying problem.

Two families of solution

ApproachIdeaUsed by
Fixed frame size + start/stop markersEvery "frame" is a known fixed length (e.g. 1 byte), bracketed by dedicated marker bitsUART: a start bit, then a fixed number of data bits, then stop bit(s)
Clock-delimited transferData is only valid while the shared clock is toggling; the receiver simply counts clock edgesSPI: the master starts and stops the clock around each transfer; I2C: explicit START and STOP conditions bracket each transaction
A UART frame: idle high, a start bit pulls the line low, then data bits, then one or more stop bits return the line high. The receiver's whole timing plan is built around locating that start-bit edge.
What "framing" buys you

Without framing, a receiver that starts listening in the middle of a byte has no way to know which bit it is looking at. UART's start bit gives the receiver an unambiguous edge to synchronize to, once, for each byte — solving the "where do I start counting" problem asynchronously, without any shared clock at all. SPI and I2C solve the same problem differently: because they carry an explicit clock, the receiver never has to guess when a bit is valid — it just watches the clock.

Error detection: parity, the simplest example

Framing tells you where a piece of data is; it says nothing about whether that data arrived correctly. The simplest layer of protection you will meet in this course is a parity bit: one extra bit, chosen so that the total number of 1s in the data-plus-parity is always even (even parity) or always odd (odd parity).

Data byte: 0110 1101   (five 1s)
Even parity bit: 1        (five 1s + this 1 = six 1s → even)   ✓
Odd parity bit:  0        (five 1s + this 0 = five 1s → odd)   ✓

The receiver recomputes the parity of what it received and compares it to the parity bit. A mismatch means at least one bit was flipped in transit — but parity cannot say which bit, and it is blind to an even number of flipped bits (two flips cancel out and parity still checks out). It is a first line of defense, cheap enough to compute in hardware on every UART, and it is exactly the "error-detection budget" every later protocol in this course spends more heavily: SPI has none built in (Chapter 3 explains why that is acceptable there), I2C has its ACK/NACK mechanism (Chapter 4), and CAN has a much stronger CRC specifically because its bus is shared and noisier (Chapter 5).

Parity checks agreement with a rule, not correctness

A parity error tells you the data is definitely wrong. But data that passes the parity check is only probably right — it is not proof. This distinction matters once you reach CAN's CRC (Chapter 5), which is far stronger than parity but is still a probabilistic check, not an absolute guarantee, against the small residual chance that random noise happens to produce a still-valid-looking frame.

Section 1.4

The physical layer: voltage levels, noise, and signaling styles

Every wire eventually meets the real, physical world: a specific voltage, a specific length of copper, and real electrical noise. Digital Logic Design covered logic levels for on-chip and short-trace signals; this section covers what changes once a signal has to travel any real distance on a cable.

Logic levels you will actually meet

Logic familyTypical supplyWhere you meet it
TTL (historic reference)5 VOlder parts, some Arduino Uno boards; VIH ≥ 2.0 V, VIL ≤ 0.8 V
CMOS at 5 V5 VClassic Arduino boards, many older sensor modules
CMOS at 3.3 V3.3 VESP32, most modern microcontrollers and sensor breakout boards
CMOS at 1.8 V1.8 VSome low-power sensor ICs, deep-submicron chips
Mixing 5 V and 3.3 V parts is the single most common wiring mistake in this whole topic

An ESP32's GPIO pins are not 5 V tolerant: driving one with a genuine 5 V signal can damage the pin permanently. Conversely, a 3.3 V output driving a 5 V-logic input's "high" threshold may not always reach it reliably. The fix is a level shifter (a simple resistor divider for one direction only, or a dedicated bidirectional level-shifter chip such as the 74LVC245 or a MOSFET-based module for two-way lines like I2C's SDA/SCL). Whenever you connect a module described as "5 V logic" to an ESP32, check this before wiring anything.

Two signaling styles: single-ended and differential

Single-endedDifferential
How a bit is representedOne wire's voltage relative to a shared groundThe voltage difference between two wires carrying opposite signals
Noise immunityLower — noise picked up by the wire directly corrupts the signalHigher — noise tends to affect both wires equally and cancels out when the receiver takes the difference
Used by, in this courseUART, SPI, I2CRS-485 and CAN (Chapter 5)

This is why RS-485 and CAN — both designed for longer cable runs in electrically noisy environments (a factory floor, a vehicle's engine bay) — use differential signaling, while the shorter, board-level protocols in Chapters 2–4 do not need to pay that extra cost.

Two failure modes worth naming now

Noise margin is the gap between a logic family's guaranteed valid "high" and "low" voltage ranges and the actual voltages a receiver requires — the buffer zone that absorbs small amounts of picked-up noise before a bit is misread. Signal reflection happens on longer or faster traces/cables when a signal's edge reaches the far end and part of it bounces back, potentially arriving back at the source and corrupting the next edge; this is why long SPI runs and any real CAN or RS-485 bus need proper termination resistors, a topic Chapter 5 covers concretely.

Why this belongs here, before any specific protocol

Every wiring mistake you will make in this topic — a level mismatch, a floating input, a bus with no pull-ups, a cable run with no termination — is a physical-layer problem, not a protocol-logic problem. Chapters 2 through 5 will each flag the specific physical-layer requirement that protocol depends on (UART's baud tolerance, SPI's clock-edge timing, I2C's open-drain pull-ups, CAN's differential termination), and all of them are specific instances of the general facts in this section.

Section 1.5

Worked problem — framing overhead and clock tolerance

Problem

A UART link runs at 250,000 bps, sending bytes framed as: 1 start bit, 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit (commonly written "8N1"). (a) Find the bit period. (b) Find the total frame length in bits and in time. (c) Find the maximum achievable throughput in bytes/second, accounting for framing overhead, and express it as a percentage of the raw bit rate. (d) If the transmitter's and receiver's clocks differ by 3%, will the last data bit of the frame still be sampled correctly? (Chapter 2 derives the exact tolerance rule; here, just reason about where the timing error has accumulated by the last bit.)

(a) T_bit = 1 / 250,000 = 4 µs

(b) Frame = 1 start + 8 data + 1 stop = 10 bits total
    Frame time = 10 × 4 µs = 40 µs

(c) In 40 µs, exactly 1 byte (8 bits of actual data) is delivered.
    Throughput = 1 byte / 40 µs = 25,000 bytes/second
    Raw bit rate equivalent: 25,000 × 8 = 200,000 bits/second of DATA
    Efficiency = 200,000 / 250,000 = 80%
    (The other 20% is the start and stop bit overhead — 2 of every 10
    bits carry no data, matching 2/10 = 20% exactly.)

(d) The receiver synchronizes fresh at the start bit's falling edge for
    EVERY byte, so timing error does not accumulate across multiple
    bytes — only within one 10-bit frame. By the last data bit (bit 9
    of 10, so 9 bit-periods after the sync edge), a 3% clock
    disagreement has caused a timing drift of about 9 × 4 µs × 0.03
    ≈ 1.08 µs, against a bit period of 4 µs. That drift (~27% of one
    bit period) is uncomfortably large but still less than the ±50%
    (half a bit period) that would cause the receiver to sample the
    wrong bit entirely — so this particular frame would likely still
    be read correctly, though with little margin left for any other
    error source. Chapter 2 will show that real UARTs budget for
    roughly 2% total clock error, not 3%, for exactly this reason.

What to take away: framing overhead is not free — here it cost a full 20% of the raw bit rate — and it is a real number you should compute whenever a system's timing budget matters, not just an abstract idea. Part (d) previews Chapter 2's central asynchronous-communication fact: because there is no shared clock, timing accuracy is a finite, spendable resource that runs out over the course of one frame and is only reset at the next start bit.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. Serial communication sends bits one after another on few wires; parallel sends many bits at once on many wires. Serial wins outside the chip because it avoids skew and scales to longer distances at lower cost — the same reason USB, SATA, and Ethernet all replaced their parallel predecessors.
  2. Bit rate (bits/second) and baud rate (symbols/second) are equal only when each symbol carries exactly one bit, which is the case for every protocol in this course. The bit period Tbit = 1/R sizes everything from receiver sampling timers to clock-tolerance budgets.
  3. Synchronous protocols (SPI, I2C) send a clock alongside the data for essentially perfect timing at the cost of an extra wire; asynchronous protocols (UART) skip the clock wire but must tolerate limited disagreement between two independent clocks.
  4. Framing marks where data starts and ends — either with dedicated start/stop bits (UART) or by tying validity to a shared clock (SPI, I2C). A parity bit gives cheap, limited error detection: it can prove data is wrong, never prove it is right.
  5. The physical layer decides whether a wire reliably carries a 1 or a 0: match logic-level voltages (5 V vs. 3.3 V vs. 1.8 V) with a level shifter where needed, and choose single-ended signaling for short board-level links or differential signaling (RS-485, CAN) for long or noisy ones. Noise margin and signal reflection are the two failure modes every wiring choice in later chapters is managing.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why did USB, SATA, and PCIe all replace earlier parallel standards, given that parallel buses send more bits per clock cycle?

At higher clock speeds, keeping many parallel wires perfectly synchronized (no skew, no crosstalk between them) became harder than simply driving one wire (or one differential pair) very fast. Serial links also scale better to longer cable runs and need far fewer, smaller connectors — advantages that outweighed the per-cycle bit-count disadvantage of parallel buses.
Question 2

A synchronous protocol and an asynchronous protocol both need to transmit bits reliably. What does each one spend to achieve that, and what does each one save?

A synchronous protocol spends an extra wire (the shared clock line) and in exchange gets essentially perfect timing, since the receiver samples exactly on each clock edge. An asynchronous protocol saves that wire but spends a strict limit on how much the transmitter's and receiver's independent clocks are allowed to disagree, since there is no shared timing reference to correct for drift.
Question 3

A byte is sent with even parity. The data byte is 1010 1100 (four 1s). What is the parity bit, and what happens if exactly two bits are corrupted in transit?

Four 1s is already even, so the even-parity bit is 0 (0 + 4 = 4, still even). If exactly two bits are flipped in transit, the total count of 1s changes by an even number, so the received data will still have an even number of 1s and will pass the parity check despite being corrupted — parity cannot detect an even number of bit flips.
Question 4

Why can't you safely wire an ESP32 GPIO pin directly to a sensor module that outputs 5 V logic?

ESP32 GPIO pins are not 5 V tolerant, so applying a genuine 5 V signal to one can damage the pin permanently. A level shifter — a simple resistor divider for a one-directional signal, or a dedicated bidirectional chip for two-way lines — is needed to bring the 5 V signal down to a safe 3.3 V level before it reaches the ESP32.
Question 5

Why do CAN and RS-485 use differential signaling while UART, SPI, and I2C do not?

CAN and RS-485 are designed for longer cable runs in electrically noisy environments, such as a vehicle's engine bay or a factory floor. Differential signaling carries the bit as the voltage difference between two wires, so noise picked up equally by both wires cancels out when the receiver takes the difference — giving much better noise immunity than single-ended signaling, which UART, SPI, and I2C don't need at their typical short, board-level distances.

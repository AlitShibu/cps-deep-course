UART

UART — Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter — is the oldest, simplest, and most forgiving serial protocol you will use, and it is almost certainly the first one you already have running: it is what carries your ESP32's Serial.print() output back to your laptop over USB. This chapter takes the framing and clock-tolerance ideas from Chapter 1 and turns them into a working link: the exact frame structure, the precise rule for how much two independent clocks are allowed to disagree, how a UART peripheral is configured and used in firmware, and the handful of wiring and buffer mistakes that account for nearly every "it's not working" UART problem.

Section 2.1

The UART frame in detail

A UART frame carries exactly one byte (in the most common configuration), bracketed by dedicated marker bits. The line idles high when nothing is being sent — this matters, because it is what makes the start of a frame detectable at all.

Idle (line high) → START bit (line pulled low) → 5-9 DATA bits (LSB first) →
[optional PARITY bit] → STOP bit(s) (line high) → back to idle (line high)
A full 8N1 UART frame (8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit): idle high, a low start bit, 8 data bits sent LSB first, then a high stop bit.

Reading the common shorthand: 8N1, 7E1, and friends

UART configurations are usually written as a three-part code: data bits, parity, stop bits.

CodeData bitsParityStop bitsWhere you'll meet it
8N18None1By far the most common default — Arduino, ESP32, most USB-serial adapters
7E17Even1Some older industrial and telecom equipment
8N28None2Occasionally used for extra timing margin on marginal links

Both ends of a link must be configured identically — same baud rate, same data bits, same parity, same stop-bit count — or every byte will be misread, even though the physical wiring is perfectly correct.

The start bit's real job

Because UART has no shared clock, the receiver's UART peripheral spends most of its time simply watching the line for a falling edge. That edge — the transition from idle-high to the start bit's low — is the only timing reference the receiver gets for the entire frame. The moment it sees that edge, the receiver's internal timer starts counting in units of Tbit, and it samples the middle of each subsequent bit period using that timer alone, with no further help from the transmitter. Section 2.2 makes precise how much error that internal timer is allowed to accumulate before it samples the wrong bit.

Why sampling happens in the MIDDLE of a bit, not at its edge

A receiver typically oversamples each bit (a common scheme uses a 16× clock) and takes its reading at the bit's midpoint rather than right at its start. This gives maximum tolerance to small timing errors in both directions — if the receiver's clock is running slightly fast or slightly slow, sampling in the middle of the bit period is the position furthest from either neighboring edge, and therefore the position least likely to be corrupted by a small timing drift.

The stop bit is not optional bookkeeping — it re-synchronizes the link

The stop bit returns the line to idle-high for at least one bit period. This guarantees the next start bit will produce a clean, unambiguous falling edge, and it gives the receiver's hardware a moment to finish processing the current byte before the next one can begin. Without it, two consecutive 0-heavy bytes could run together with no clear boundary between them.

Section 2.2

How much clock disagreement UART can tolerate

Chapter 1's worked problem estimated, informally, that a UART frame can tolerate some clock disagreement but not unlimited disagreement. This section derives the exact rule.

Setting up the calculation

The receiver synchronizes once, at the falling edge of the start bit, and then free-runs its own clock for the rest of the frame. By the time it samples the last bit of an N-bit frame (counting the start bit as bit 0), it has been N − 1 bit periods since synchronization. If the transmitter's and receiver's clocks disagree by a fractional error ε (for example, ε = 0.02 for 2%), the accumulated timing error by the last bit is:

Δt = (N − 1) · T_bit · ε

For the receiver to still sample the correct bit, this accumulated error must stay within half a bit period of the ideal sampling point (if the drift exceeds half a bit period, the receiver's sample lands inside the wrong bit entirely):

(N − 1) · T_bit · ε  <  T_bit / 2
ε  <  1 / [ 2(N − 1) ]                      (the maximum tolerable clock error)
Applying it to a standard 8N1 frame 
Frame: 1 start + 8 data + 1 stop = 10 bits, so N = 10, N − 1 = 9

ε_max = 1 / (2 × 9) = 1/18 ≈ 0.0556 → about 5.6%

That is the theoretical ceiling for a single 10-bit frame with ideal midpoint sampling and no other error sources. In practice, designers budget for far less — typically around 2% total combined error — to leave margin for the receiver's own oversampling-clock quantization error, any asymmetry in how the transmitter times its bits, and simple engineering caution. This is exactly why Chapter 1's worked problem flagged a 3% clock mismatch as "uncomfortably large... though this particular frame would likely still be read correctly" — 3% is inside the ~5.6% hard ceiling but outside the ~2% margin real designs actually target.

Where clock error actually comes from

Both the microcontroller's UART peripheral and the far-end device (another microcontroller, a USB-serial adapter, a GPS module) generate their baud rate by dividing down a crystal oscillator or an internal RC oscillator. Two sources of error stack up:

  • Oscillator accuracy. A crystal is typically accurate to within 20–50 ppm (0.002–0.005%) — essentially perfect for this purpose. An internal RC oscillator (used when a part has no external crystal) can be off by 1–2% or more, and drifts further with temperature.
  • Divider rounding. A UART generates its baud rate by dividing a fixed clock frequency by an integer. If the division does not come out to a whole number, the actual baud rate is rounded to the nearest achievable value, introducing a small additional fixed error — often under 1% for common baud rates on common clock frequencies, but worth checking for unusual combinations.
Two devices at the "same" baud rate can still disagree

Two boards both configured for "9600 baud" are not necessarily transmitting at identical rates — each one's actual rate depends on its own oscillator's real frequency and its own divider rounding. This is almost always fine, because both effects together usually stay well under the ~2% design margin. It becomes a real problem only when one side uses an inaccurate internal RC oscillator for its UART clock, which is why datasheets for such parts specifically call out their baud-rate accuracy at each supported rate.

Section 2.3

UART peripherals and firmware

On a microcontroller, sending and receiving UART data is almost never done by manually toggling a GPIO pin — a dedicated hardware peripheral handles the bit-level timing, and firmware works at the level of whole bytes.

How the hardware peripheral works

On transmit, firmware writes a byte into a hardware register; the UART peripheral automatically shifts it out bit by bit, at the configured baud rate, adding the start bit, parity (if enabled), and stop bit(s) with no further CPU involvement. On receive, the peripheral watches the RX pin, detects the start-bit edge, samples each bit at the configured rate, strips the framing bits, and places the resulting byte into a receive register or a small hardware FIFO buffer — again without the CPU needing to babysit individual bits.

Arduino-style code

void setup() {
    Serial.begin(115200);           // configure UART0, 115200 baud, default 8N1
}

void loop() {
    if (Serial.available() > 0) {   // has the hardware FIFO received a byte?
        int b = Serial.read();      // pull one byte out of the FIFO
        Serial.print("Got: ");
        Serial.println(b);
    }
}

ESP-IDF style (a second, independent hardware UART)

#include "driver/uart.h"

#define UART_NUM      UART_NUM_1
#define TXD_PIN       17
#define RXD_PIN       16
#define BUF_SIZE      1024

void uart_setup(void) {
    uart_config_t cfg = {
        .baud_rate = 9600,
        .data_bits = UART_DATA_8_BITS,
        .parity    = UART_PARITY_DISABLE,
        .stop_bits = UART_STOP_BITS_1,
        .flow_ctrl = UART_HW_FLOWCTRL_DISABLE,
    };
    uart_param_config(UART_NUM, &cfg);
    uart_set_pin(UART_NUM, TXD_PIN, RXD_PIN, UART_PIN_NO_CHANGE, UART_PIN_NO_CHANGE);
    uart_driver_install(UART_NUM, BUF_SIZE * 2, 0, 0, NULL, 0);
}

void uart_send_and_check(void) {
    const char *msg = "hello\n";
    uart_write_bytes(UART_NUM, msg, strlen(msg));

    uint8_t data[BUF_SIZE];
    int len = uart_read_bytes(UART_NUM, data, BUF_SIZE, pdMS_TO_TICKS(20));
    if (len > 0) {
        // process len bytes in data[]
    }
}
Why a receive buffer (FIFO) matters

Bytes can arrive faster than your main loop gets around to reading them — especially if the loop is busy doing something else for a few milliseconds. The hardware FIFO (and the driver's larger software buffer, as configured in uart_driver_install above) absorbs that mismatch by holding received bytes until the firmware calls a read function. If the buffer fills up before firmware reads it, new incoming bytes are lost — this is a buffer overrun, and it is one of the most common real UART bugs, especially at high baud rates or in code with long blocking operations between reads.

Section 2.4

Flow control, break conditions, and RS-232

Beyond the two most common configurations, three ideas are worth knowing because you will meet them on real hardware and in real datasheets.

Hardware flow control: RTS/CTS

Plain UART has no built-in way for a slow receiver to tell a fast transmitter "wait, I'm not ready." Adding two extra wires — RTS (Request To Send) and CTS (Clear To Send) — lets each side signal the other when it is (or is not) ready to accept more data, preventing buffer overruns at the hardware level rather than relying on the receiver always being fast enough. It costs two additional pins and is only used when a link genuinely needs it — most short, simple UART links (sensor to microcontroller, debug console) skip it entirely.

Break conditions

A break condition is the line held low for longer than a full frame would take — longer than any valid start bit followed by data could ever be. Because it cannot be confused with normal data, it is used as a special out-of-band signal: some bootloaders use a break condition to force a device into a firmware-update mode, and some protocols use it to signal a reset or a special "attention" condition to the receiver.

RS-232 vs. TTL-level UART: same protocol, different voltages

The UART protocol (the framing from Section 2.1) is separate from the voltage levels used to carry it. TTL-level UART (what a microcontroller's pins produce directly) uses 0 V and 3.3 V or 5 V. Classic RS-232 (the "serial port" on old PCs) uses much larger swings — historically ±12 V — and inverts the logic sense (a logic 1 is a negative voltage). A USB-to-serial adapter, or a level converter chip such as the MAX232, translates between the two; connecting a microcontroller's TTL UART pins directly to a true RS-232 port without such a converter will not communicate correctly and can damage the microcontroller pin.

The most common wiring mistake of all: TX/RX crossed or matched the wrong way

A device's TX (transmit) pin must connect to the other device's RX (receive) pin, and vice versa — TX to RX, RX to TX, crossed, not TX-to-TX. This trips up nearly everyone the first time, precisely because every other common wire (power, ground, I2C's SDA, SPI's MOSI on a matching bus) is connected "straight across," name to matching name. If two devices seem to send but never receive anything from each other, this is the first thing to check, before suspecting the baud rate, the voltage levels, or the code.

Section 2.5

Worked problem — clock tolerance and a configuration mismatch

Problem

An ESP32 (using an accurate crystal oscillator, negligible clock error) talks to a sensor module whose datasheet specifies its internal UART clock is accurate to only ±2.5%. Both are configured 8N1 at 19200 baud. (a) Is this combination safe, using the exact tolerance formula? (b) The sensor's firmware is later updated to send 8E2 frames (8 data, even parity, 2 stop bits) at the same baud rate, without telling the ESP32 side, which stays at 8N1. What happens? (c) Suppose instead both sides correctly agree on 8E2 — does the extra parity and stop bit change the clock-tolerance calculation, and if so, how?

(a) Frame: 1 start + 8 data + 1 stop = 10 bits → N = 10, N − 1 = 9
    ε_max = 1 / (2 × 9) = 1/18 ≈ 5.56%

    Combined clock error ≈ 2.5% (ESP32's crystal error is negligible
    by comparison) is well under the 5.56% hard ceiling, and also
    under the ~2% recommended design margin only by a small amount —
    workable, but with less safety margin than a careful design would
    prefer. In practice this specific link would very likely work
    reliably, since 2.5% is still comfortably below the hard limit,
    but it is not the generous margin a designer would choose from
    scratch.

(b) The two sides now disagree about frame structure, not just clock
    rate. The ESP32 (expecting 8N1: 10 bits/frame) will misinterpret
    the sensor's 8E2 frames (1 start + 8 data + 1 parity + 2 stop =
    12 bits/frame): it will treat the sensor's parity bit and part of
    its first stop bit as if they were the start of a NEW frame,
    producing a stream of garbled, meaningless bytes. This is a
    configuration mismatch, not a timing problem, and no amount of
    clock accuracy fixes it — both sides must be manually reconfigured
    to agree.

(c) Yes. With 8E2, the frame is now 1 + 8 + 1 + 2 = 12 bits, so
    N = 12, N − 1 = 11:
    ε_max = 1 / (2 × 11) = 1/22 ≈ 4.55%
    A LONGER frame gives the receiver's clock MORE time to drift
    before the last bit is sampled, so it REDUCES the tolerable
    clock error compared to the shorter 8N1 frame's 5.56% ceiling.

What to take away: part (b) is the single most instructive result in this problem — a clock-accuracy issue and a configuration-mismatch issue produce completely different symptoms (subtly marginal timing vs. total garbage), and diagnosing which one you are looking at is a real, practical skill. Part (c) generalizes Section 2.2's derivation: any feature that lengthens the frame (more data bits, parity, extra stop bits) tightens the clock-tolerance requirement, because it gives the free-running receiver clock more time to drift before the frame ends.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. A UART frame is idle-high, then a low start bit, then 5–9 data bits (LSB first), an optional parity bit, and 1–2 stop bits, back to idle-high. Configurations are named by a code like 8N1 (8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit); both ends of a link must match exactly.
  2. The start bit's falling edge is the receiver's only timing reference for the whole frame; it samples each bit at its midpoint for maximum tolerance to drift. The stop bit guarantees a clean edge for the next frame's start bit.
  3. The exact clock-tolerance rule: εmax = 1/[2(N−1)] for an N-bit frame. A standard 8N1 frame (N=10) tolerates up to ≈5.6% clock disagreement in theory; real designs target roughly 2% to leave margin. Longer frames (more data bits, parity, extra stop bits) tolerate LESS clock error, not more.
  4. In firmware, a hardware UART peripheral handles bit-level timing automatically; the CPU works with whole bytes via a hardware/software FIFO buffer. A buffer overrun (bytes lost because firmware didn't read the FIFO in time) is the most common software-side UART bug.
  5. RTS/CTS flow control lets a receiver signal readiness at the hardware level; a break condition is a deliberately-too-long low period used as an out-of-band signal; RS-232 uses the same framing as TTL UART but with different, inverted voltage levels and needs a converter (e.g. MAX232) to interface safely.
  6. The single most common UART wiring mistake is connecting TX to TX and RX to RX instead of crossing them (TX→RX, RX→TX) — check this before suspecting baud rate or voltage levels.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why does the receiver sample each UART bit at its MIDDLE rather than immediately after the previous bit ends?

Sampling at the midpoint gives the maximum possible distance from either neighboring bit edge, so it is the position most tolerant of small timing drift in either direction (the receiver's clock running slightly fast or slightly slow). Sampling right at an edge would leave almost no margin before a small timing error caused the wrong bit to be read.
Question 2

Using the exact formula, what is the maximum tolerable clock error for an 8N2 frame (8 data bits, no parity, 2 stop bits)?

Frame length: 1 start + 8 data + 2 stop = 11 bits, so N = 11 and N − 1 = 10. ε_max = 1/(2×10) = 1/20 = 5%. This is slightly tighter than the standard 8N1 frame's ≈5.56%, because the extra stop bit lengthens the frame and gives the receiver's clock more time to drift before the last bit is sampled — though 8N2 is usually chosen for extra timing margin on the RECEIVING side's re-synchronization, not because it improves clock tolerance.
Question 3

Two devices are both configured for '38400 baud, 8N1' but never communicate correctly — every received byte is garbage. List two categories of root cause this could be (not specific fixes), and how you'd distinguish them.

One category is a physical/wiring issue — for example, TX and RX connected straight across instead of crossed, or a logic-level mismatch (5 V vs. 3.3 V) that the receiver can't reliably read. Another category is a configuration mismatch beyond just the stated baud rate — for example, one side actually using parity or a different stop-bit count despite both being described as '8N1' by mistake. Wiring issues typically show as ALL received data being garbage or nothing received at all, while a subtle clock-tolerance issue (as opposed to full mismatch) would show as occasional, intermittent errors rather than every single byte being wrong.
Question 4

Why does connecting a microcontroller's TTL-level UART pins directly to a classic RS-232 port risk damaging the microcontroller?

Classic RS-232 uses much larger voltage swings, historically around ±12 V, and inverts the logic sense compared to TTL levels. A microcontroller's GPIO pins are only rated for a few volts (typically 3.3 V or 5 V), so the ±12 V RS-232 signal would exceed the pin's absolute maximum voltage rating and can permanently damage it. A level converter chip such as the MAX232 is needed to translate safely between the two voltage domains.
Question 5

A frame is lengthened by adding a parity bit, going from 8N1 to 8E1. Does this make the link MORE or LESS tolerant of clock disagreement between transmitter and receiver, and why?

It makes the link LESS tolerant. Going from 8N1 (10 bits, N−1=9) to 8E1 (11 bits: 1 start + 8 data + 1 parity + 1 stop, N−1=10) increases N−1 in the formula ε_max = 1/[2(N−1)], which decreases ε_max (from about 5.56% down to 5%). A longer frame gives the receiver's free-running clock more time to accumulate drift before the final bit is sampled, so the tolerable clock error shrinks.

I2C

I2C (Inter-Integrated Circuit, sometimes written I²C or IIC) solves the exact problem Chapter 3 left unsolved elegantly: talking to many devices without a dedicated wire per device. It does this with just two shared wires, an addressing scheme built into every transaction, and a strange-looking output stage — open-drain with pull-up resistors — that turns out to be the key to letting many devices share the same two wires safely. This chapter covers START/STOP conditions, 7-bit and 10-bit addressing, the ACK/NACK acknowledgment built into every byte, clock stretching, multi-master arbitration, and the pull-up resistor sizing that so often trips up a first I2C design.

Section 4.1

The two-wire bus and open-drain outputs

I2C uses exactly two wires, shared by every device on the bus, master or slave alike:

SignalFull nameRole
SDASerial DataCarries every bit of data, in both directions, shared by all devices
SCLSerial ClockCarries the clock signal, normally driven by the master, shared by all devices

Compare this immediately to Chapter 3: SPI needed 3 shared wires plus 1 dedicated wire per slave. I2C needs exactly 2 wires, full stop, no matter whether there is 1 slave or 100 — the trade Section 3.3 already flagged as coming.

The mechanism that makes sharing possible: open-drain outputs

A normal digital output can actively drive a pin to both logic high (connecting it to the supply voltage) and logic low (connecting it to ground). An open-drain output can only actively pull the line LOW — it can never actively drive it high. To get a high level, the line relies entirely on a pull-up resistor connected from the wire to the supply voltage, which passively pulls the line back up whenever no device is actively pulling it low.

No device pulling low  →  pull-up resistor wins  →  line reads HIGH
Any device pulling low →  that device wins        →  line reads LOW
An open-drain bus: any single device can pull the shared line low, but only the pull-up resistor can bring it back high once everyone releases it.
Why open-drain, specifically — what it makes possible

If every device used a normal push-pull output (able to actively drive both high and low, like SPI's outputs), then two devices disagreeing about the line's state at the same moment would fight — one trying to drive it high while another drives it low — a direct short circuit that could damage both outputs. Open-drain sidesteps this entirely: since no device can ever actively drive the line high, the worst that can happen is multiple devices simultaneously pulling it low, which is perfectly safe (the line simply reads low, exactly as if only one device had pulled it low). This is precisely what makes it safe for many devices to share the same two wires without any device needing to know, in advance, whether another device might be driving the line at the same instant — and it is also the mechanism behind both clock stretching (Section 4.4) and safe multi-master arbitration (Section 4.5).

Sizing the pull-up resistor: a real trade-off, not a fixed value

Because the pull-up resistor is what pulls the line back high, its value directly trades off two competing effects:

  • Too large a resistor: the line rises too slowly (an RC-charging effect against the bus's own parasitic capacitance — from Basic Electronics, this is exactly a charging capacitor's time constant), limiting how fast the bus can reliably run, especially with more devices or longer wires adding more capacitance.
  • Too small a resistor: more current flows whenever any device pulls the line low, wasting power and, in extreme cases, exceeding a device's rated current-sink capability.

Typical values are in the 2 kΩ–10 kΩ range for standard-speed I2C (100 kHz) at 3.3 V or 5 V, with smaller values (stronger pull-ups) needed for faster bus speeds or buses with many devices and longer traces. Many I2C sensor breakout boards include their own onboard pull-up resistors — if you connect two or more such boards to the same bus, you end up with several pull-ups in parallel, which combine to make the effective resistance lower than intended and can, in extreme cases with many boards, pull the resistance low enough to cause signal integrity problems.

The single most common I2C wiring mistake: no pull-up resistors at all

If a bus has no pull-up resistors anywhere on it — no board-provided ones and none added externally — the SDA and SCL lines will never be reliably pulled high, and the bus simply will not work: reads and writes will fail or return garbage, in a way that can look like a code or addressing problem when the actual cause is purely electrical. Before debugging I2C code, always confirm pull-ups exist somewhere on the bus (checking the sensor board's schematic, or simply measuring the idle voltage on SDA/SCL with a multimeter, which should read close to the supply voltage when the bus is idle).

Section 4.2

START, STOP, and device addressing

Where SPI uses a dedicated CS wire to select a device (Chapter 3), and UART has no concept of multiple devices sharing a line at all (Chapter 2), I2C solves addressing entirely within the data stream itself, using two special bus conditions that could never be confused with ordinary data.

START and STOP conditions

Ordinarily, SDA is only allowed to change while SCL is low (changing it while SCL is high, in the middle of a normal data bit, would be ambiguous). I2C deliberately breaks this rule exactly twice, to create two unambiguous signals:

START condition:  SDA transitions HIGH → LOW while SCL is HIGH
STOP  condition:  SDA transitions LOW → HIGH while SCL is HIGH

Because ordinary data transitions never happen while SCL is high, these two specific transitions can never be mistaken for data — they are how every device on the bus recognizes "a transaction is beginning" (START) or "a transaction has ended, the bus is now free" (STOP), functioning as I2C's version of Chapter 1's framing markers, but built from the physical signal itself rather than from extra bits sent over the wire.

The two special conditions I2C is built on: SDA changing while SCL is high is forbidden everywhere except these two moments.

Addressing: 7-bit (and 10-bit)

Immediately after a START condition, the master sends the address of the device it wants to talk to. The standard address is 7 bits, followed by a single Read/Write bit — 8 bits total, sent as one byte:

[ 7-bit device address ][ R/W bit ]
   bit7 ... bit1           bit0

R/W = 0  → the master intends to WRITE to the addressed device
R/W = 1  → the master intends to READ from the addressed device

Every device on the bus listens to this address byte; only the device whose own address matches responds (with an ACK, Section 4.3) — every other device stays silent and ignores the rest of the transaction until the next START or STOP condition. A 7-bit address space allows 128 possible addresses, though a handful are reserved for special bus operations, leaving 112 usable addresses — usually more than enough, though 10-bit addressing exists as an extension for the rare case where more distinct devices need to coexist on one bus.

Two identical sensor modules on one bus is the classic I2C address collision

Many sensor chips have a fixed factory address (or an address selectable only among 2–4 options via a hardware pin). Connecting two identical modules with the same fixed address to the same bus means the master cannot distinguish between them — both will respond to the same address, and the resulting behavior is undefined and unreliable. The usual fixes are: choosing modules whose address can be changed via a solder jumper or address pin, using an I2C multiplexer chip to place each identical device on its own isolated sub-bus, or simply choosing a different, address-configurable sensor for one of the two roles.

Section 4.3

Acknowledgment: ACK and NACK on every byte

Every single byte transferred over I2C — the address byte and every data byte after it — is followed by one extra clock pulse dedicated entirely to acknowledgment. This is a fundamental structural difference from both UART (whose optional parity bit, Chapter 1, only checks correctness after the fact, for the whole byte) and SPI (which has no acknowledgment mechanism at all).

Each byte:  [ 8 data bits ][ 1 ACK/NACK bit ]

After the 8th data bit, the SENDER releases SDA (lets it float high),
and the RECEIVER pulls SDA low for that 9th clock pulse to say
"I received that byte" — this is an ACK (Acknowledge).

If the receiver does NOT pull SDA low (leaves it high) during that
9th pulse, this is a NACK (Not Acknowledged) — the sender knows
the byte was not accepted, for one of several possible reasons.
What a NACK actually tells you, in three different situations

A NACK after the address byte usually means no device on the bus recognized that address — either it's the wrong address, or that device is not physically present/powered. A NACK after a data byte during a write can mean the receiving device's internal buffer is full, or that it's rejecting the value for some reason specific to that device. A master reading data from a slave sends its own NACK deliberately, on the very last byte it wants to receive, as the standard way of telling the slave "stop sending, I'm done" — the only case in this list where a NACK is the expected, correct outcome rather than an error signal.

ACK/NACK in firmware, at the level you actually see

On virtually every microcontroller platform, the I2C driver library handles ACK/NACK entirely automatically at the byte level — you almost never toggle it by hand. What you do see is the function call's return value or error code reporting whether the device acknowledged:

#include <Wire.h>

void setup() {
    Wire.begin();               // join the I2C bus as master
}

bool sensor_present(uint8_t addr) {
    Wire.beginTransmission(addr);   // sends START + address byte internally
    uint8_t result = Wire.endTransmission();  // sends STOP, returns ACK/NACK status
    return (result == 0);           // 0 means the device ACKed — it's present
}

This exact pattern — attempting a zero-length write to every possible address and checking which ones ACK — is how a standard "I2C bus scanner" utility works, and it is one of the most useful first debugging steps whenever a new I2C device does not seem to respond: it tells you definitively whether the device is electrically present and responding at all, before you worry about anything in your actual application code.

Section 4.4

Clock stretching and multi-master arbitration

Clock stretching

Normally the master drives SCL, setting the pace for the whole transaction. But because SCL is also open-drain (Section 4.1), a slave can, if it needs more time before it is ready to send or receive the next bit, hold SCL low itself, even after the master has released it. The master, watching the line, sees it is still low and simply waits — it cannot proceed until the slave releases SCL back to its pulled-up high state. This is clock stretching, and it is a direct, elegant consequence of SCL sharing the exact same open-drain mechanism as SDA: nothing new had to be invented for slaves to be able to pause the bus, they simply exercise the same "pull it low" capability every device already has.

Why this matters for slow sensors and simple microcontrollers

A slave built around a slow internal process (an ADC conversion still finishing, a small microcontroller busy with something else) can use clock stretching to avoid needing to buffer an entire response in advance or risk missing bits it isn't ready to send yet. Not every I2C peripheral supports issuing clock stretches (some simple microcontroller I2C hardware peripherals, running as a slave, cannot stretch the clock at all), and not every master correctly handles a stretched clock (a few cheap or misconfigured master implementations use a fixed-duration timeout and may misbehave if a slave stretches for too long) — so it is worth checking both ends' documentation on a design where clock stretching might be needed.

Multi-master arbitration

I2C uniquely permits more than one device to act as a master, potentially even starting transactions at the same time — and it resolves the resulting conflict without any device needing to ask permission first, using a mechanism built directly on the same open-drain logic.

Rule: while transmitting, every master continuously reads back what is
actually on SDA and compares it to what it intended to send.

If a master intended to send a 1 (meaning: release SDA, let the
pull-up bring it high) but reads back a 0 (someone else is pulling
it low), that master has LOST arbitration — it immediately stops
driving the bus and becomes a listener for the rest of that
transaction.

The master that intended to send a 0 at that same bit "wins" —
completely without needing to know in advance that a collision
was even happening.
Why lower addresses (and 0-bits generally) always win arbitration

Because a 0 always overrides a 1 on an open-drain bus (any device pulling low wins, regardless of what any other device intended), a master sending an address with more leading 0-bits at the point of disagreement will always win arbitration over one sending a 1 at that same bit position. In practice, this means devices with numerically lower I2C addresses have a structural priority advantage in the rare event of simultaneous multi-master transmission — a subtle but direct consequence of nothing more exotic than the open-drain rule from Section 4.1.

Section 4.5

I2C in ESP-IDF-style firmware

#include "driver/i2c.h"

#define I2C_PORT      I2C_NUM_0
#define SDA_PIN       21
#define SCL_PIN       22
#define SENSOR_ADDR   0x48

void i2c_setup(void) {
    i2c_config_t cfg = {
        .mode = I2C_MODE_MASTER,
        .sda_io_num = SDA_PIN,
        .scl_io_num = SCL_PIN,
        .sda_pullup_en = GPIO_PULLUP_ENABLE,   // internal pull-ups (use external ones for reliability)
        .scl_pullup_en = GPIO_PULLUP_ENABLE,
        .master.clk_speed = 100000,            // 100 kHz, standard mode
    };
    i2c_param_config(I2C_PORT, &cfg);
    i2c_driver_install(I2C_PORT, cfg.mode, 0, 0, 0);
}

esp_err_t read_register(uint8_t reg, uint8_t *value) {
    // Write the register address, then read one byte back — a very
    // common two-part I2C transaction pattern for sensor registers.
    i2c_cmd_handle_t cmd = i2c_cmd_link_create();
    i2c_master_start(cmd);
    i2c_master_write_byte(cmd, (SENSOR_ADDR << 1) | I2C_MASTER_WRITE, true);
    i2c_master_write_byte(cmd, reg, true);
    i2c_master_start(cmd);   // a "repeated start" — no STOP before this
    i2c_master_write_byte(cmd, (SENSOR_ADDR << 1) | I2C_MASTER_READ, true);
    i2c_master_read_byte(cmd, value, I2C_MASTER_NACK);   // NACK: this is the last byte
    i2c_master_stop(cmd);
    esp_err_t result = i2c_master_cmd_begin(I2C_PORT, cmd, pdMS_TO_TICKS(100));
    i2c_cmd_link_delete(cmd);
    return result;
}
The repeated START: combining a write and a read without releasing the bus

The code above issues a second START condition without a STOP in between — a repeated start. This is how the overwhelmingly common "write a register address, then read that register's value" pattern is done in I2C: it tells every other device on the bus that this is still one continuous transaction (so no other master can start a new one in between), while still letting the master switch the R/W bit from write to read partway through. If a STOP were issued between the two parts instead, another master could in principle start its own transaction with a different device in the gap — the repeated start guarantees the read happens immediately, addressing the same device, uninterrupted.

Internal pull-ups are often too weak for a real bus

Many microcontrollers offer internal pull-up resistors on their I2C-capable pins (as enabled in the code above), but these are typically tens of kΩ — far weaker than the 2–10 kΩ range Section 4.1 recommended for a reliable bus, especially with more than one or two devices or any real length of wire. Internal pull-ups are fine for a quick single-sensor bench test; a design meant to actually work reliably should almost always add external pull-up resistors of an appropriate value.

Section 4.6

Worked problem — a repeated-start register read and an address collision

Problem

An ESP32 needs to read a temperature value from a sensor at I2C address 0x48. The sensor's temperature value lives in register 0x00, is 2 bytes wide, and the sensor requires a repeated-start read (write the register address, then read 2 bytes without releasing the bus). (a) Write out the full sequence of bus conditions and bytes, including which bit is the R/W bit in each address byte. (b) The master's I2C scan shows no device ACKs at address 0x48, but the sensor is definitely powered and wired correctly according to its datasheet. Name two categories of likely cause. (c) Suppose the bus also has a second, identical sensor module wired in parallel, with no way to change its address. What is the specific fix, and why won't simply "trying to talk to it separately in code" work?

(a) START
    Address byte:  (0x48 << 1) | 0 = 0x90   (R/W=0: WRITE)  → expect ACK
    Data byte:     0x00                      (register address) → expect ACK
    REPEATED START (no STOP)
    Address byte:  (0x48 << 1) | 1 = 0x91   (R/W=1: READ)   → expect ACK
    Data byte:     [high byte of temperature] ← sensor sends → master ACKs
                   (master ACKs because it wants MORE bytes)
    Data byte:     [low byte of temperature]  ← sensor sends → master NACKs
                   (master NACKs: "that's the last byte I want")
    STOP

(b) Category 1 — electrical/wiring: no pull-up resistors present
    anywhere on the bus (Section 4.1's most common real mistake), a
    swapped SDA/SCL connection, or a voltage-level mismatch between
    the sensor's logic level and the ESP32's (Chapter 1, Section 1.4).
    Category 2 — addressing: the sensor's TRUE address may not
    actually be 0x48 as assumed — some datasheets specify the 8-bit
    write-address (already including the R/W bit, i.e. 0x90) where
    the code expects the bare 7-bit address (0x48), a very common
    off-by-one-bit-shift confusion when reading datasheets.

(c) The specific fix is Section 4.2's stated solution: use an I2C
    multiplexer chip to place each identical sensor on its own
    isolated sub-bus, or replace one sensor with a variant whose
    address can be changed via a hardware pin/jumper. Simply "trying
    to talk to it separately in code" cannot work because the
    ambiguity is PHYSICAL, not logical — when the master sends
    address 0x48 on the shared bus, BOTH identical sensors see and
    respond to that same address at the same time (both driving SDA
    for their ACK, and both attempting to shift out their own
    temperature data on the same wire during the read) — there is no
    way for code running on the master to selectively address only
    one of two physically identical devices sharing the same wire and
    the same address, no matter how the software is written.

What to take away: part (a) is the concrete shape of the repeated-start pattern that Section 4.5's code implements, worth being able to write out by hand. Part (c) makes a point worth remembering generally: some I2C problems are fundamentally about the physical bus topology (two identical, unaddressable devices sharing one bus) and genuinely cannot be solved in software, no matter how the code is restructured — the fix has to be electrical (a multiplexer, or a hardware address-select pin).

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. I2C uses exactly two shared wires (SDA, SCL) regardless of device count, using open-drain outputs: any device can pull a line low, but only a pull-up resistor brings it back high. This makes sharing safe (no output-fighting) and enables both clock stretching and multi-master arbitration for free.
  2. A START condition (SDA falls while SCL is high) begins a transaction; a STOP (SDA rises while SCL is high) ends one. A 7-bit address plus an R/W bit follows every START, and only the matching device responds — this is I2C's built-in addressing, unlike SPI's dedicated CS wires.
  3. Every byte, including the address byte, is followed by an ACK/NACK bit: the receiver pulls SDA low to acknowledge. A NACK means different things in different contexts — no device present, a receiver rejecting data, or (during a master's read) a deliberate 'stop sending' signal.
  4. Clock stretching lets a slave pause the bus by holding SCL low, using the same open-drain mechanism as data. Multi-master arbitration resolves simultaneous transmissions bit-by-bit with no prior coordination needed: whichever master sends a 0 where another sends a 1 automatically wins, since 0 always overrides 1 on an open-drain bus.
  5. Standard mode is 100 kHz, Fast mode 400 kHz, with faster modes existing but less universal. A repeated start (a second START with no STOP in between) lets a master write a register address then read its value as one uninterrupted transaction. Two identical, unaddressable devices on one bus is a genuine hardware-level conflict that no software fix can resolve — it needs a multiplexer or an address-select pin.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why is it electrically safe for two I2C devices to pull SDA low at the exact same moment, when it would be dangerous for two normal push-pull outputs to do the same on a shared wire?

I2C's open-drain outputs can only ever actively pull the line low, never actively drive it high — so two devices pulling low simultaneously simply agree that the line should be low, with no conflict. Two push-pull outputs disagreeing (one driving high, one driving low) would instead create a direct low-impedance path between the supply and ground through both output stages, which could damage them — open-drain avoids this failure mode entirely by removing any device's ability to actively drive the line high.
Question 2

A master sends address byte 0x91 at the start of a transaction. What is the 7-bit device address being addressed, and is this a read or a write?

0x91 in binary is 1001 0001. The last bit (bit 0) is the R/W bit, which is 1, meaning this is a READ. The remaining 7 bits (1001000, i.e. 0x48) are the device address, so this addresses device 0x48 for a read operation — matching the read half of Section 4.6's worked example.
Question 3

A slave device needs extra time to prepare data before continuing a transaction. What mechanism does it use, and what property of the I2C bus makes that mechanism possible in the first place?

The slave uses clock stretching: it holds the SCL line low even after the master has released it, forcing the master to wait until the slave releases SCL back to high. This is possible because SCL, like SDA, is an open-drain line — any device sharing the bus, including a slave, has the same fundamental ability to pull it low, so no special new hardware capability is needed for a slave to pause the clock.
Question 4

Two masters begin transmitting on the same I2C bus at the same time. One intends to send a 1 at a certain bit position; the other intends to send a 0 at that same position. What happens, and why?

The master intending to send a 0 wins arbitration, because on an open-drain bus a 0 (a device actively pulling the line low) always overrides a 1 (a device merely releasing the line and relying on the pull-up). The master that intended the 1 reads back a 0 instead of the 1 it expected, recognizes it has lost arbitration, and immediately stops driving the bus — becoming a listener for the rest of that transaction, with no prior coordination between the two masters required.
Question 5

What specific problem does a repeated START solve that could not be achieved by simply issuing a STOP after the write and then a fresh START before the read?

Issuing a STOP releases the bus entirely, meaning another master (in a multi-master system) could, in principle, begin its own transaction with a different device in the gap before the read's fresh START arrives. A repeated START keeps the bus continuously occupied by the current master's transaction from the write straight through to the read of the same device, guaranteeing no other transaction can be interleaved in between — which matters for reading a register value that must correspond to the exact register address just written.

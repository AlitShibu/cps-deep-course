Choosing and Debugging Protocols

Chapters 2 through 5 gave you four separate protocols, each with its own rules. Real design work asks a different kind of question: given a specific sensor, display, or set of constraints, which protocol should you reach for — and once it's wired up and not working, how do you actually find out why? This chapter turns the whole topic into a decision tool: a structured way to choose between UART, SPI, I2C, and CAN/RS-485 for a given job, how to read a logic analyzer or oscilloscope capture of each protocol, and a systematic checklist for the wiring and configuration mistakes that account for the overwhelming majority of real serial-communication bugs.

Section 6.1

A decision framework: which protocol for which job

There is no single "best" protocol — each one was designed around a different set of priorities, and Chapters 2–5 showed exactly what each one trades away to get what it optimizes for. Choosing well means identifying which trade-off actually matches your situation.

Three quick real scenarios

A single temperature sensor next to the microcontroller, pins are not scarce: SPI if the sensor supports it and you want maximum speed/simplicity per transfer; I2C if you'd rather save pins and speed doesn't matter. Six different sensors on a small PCB, pins ARE scarce: I2C, almost without question — six SPI devices would need 6 dedicated CS pins plus 3 shared ones (9 total, Chapter 3's Section 3.3 table), while I2C needs exactly 2 no matter how many sensors share the bus, provided none of them share the same fixed address. A sensor on the far side of a large outdoor installation, 200 meters away, near heavy equipment: RS-485 or CAN — I2C and SPI's distance and noise-immunity limits (Chapters 3–4) rule them out entirely; this is exactly the long-distance, noisy-environment case Chapter 5 was built for.

A pattern worth noticing across all four protocols

Every one of these trade-offs traces back to something built into the earlier chapters: UART's speed ceiling is the clock-tolerance formula from Section 2.2; SPI's per-device pin cost is the direct consequence of using dedicated CS lines instead of addressing (Section 3.3); I2C's lower practical speed ceiling comes from its open-drain pull-up mechanism (Section 4.1); CAN/RS-485's long reach comes from differential signaling (Section 5.1). None of these are arbitrary design quirks — each is the specific, traceable cost of that protocol's specific, deliberate advantage.

Section 6.2

Reading a logic analyzer capture

A logic analyzer captures the digital high/low state of several pins over time and displays them as waveforms — it is, for digital serial debugging, the single most valuable tool you can own, because it shows you exactly what is actually happening on the wires, rather than what your code believes should be happening. An oscilloscope shows similar timing information but as continuous analog voltage, which matters when you suspect an electrical-level problem (a signal that isn't reaching full logic levels, excessive ringing, or slow rise/fall times) rather than a purely logical one.

What each protocol's capture should look like

ProtocolWhat a healthy capture shows
UARTA single line idling high, a clean low start bit, 8 data bits at a constant, calculable bit period (Chapter 2, Section 2.1), returning to idle-high for the stop bit — most logic analyzer software can decode this into actual byte values automatically if you tell it the baud rate
SPICS going low, a clean, regular clock train on SCLK for the duration of the transfer, data on MOSI/MISO changing and being sampled on the correct edges for the configured mode (Chapter 3, Section 3.2), CS returning high
I2CA START condition (SDA falling while SCL is high), an address byte with its ACK bit, data bytes each with their own ACK bit, and a STOP condition (SDA rising while SCL is high) — most analyzer software decodes the address and R/W bit automatically
CANAn identifier field, visible bit-stuffing (an occasional extra bit breaking up long runs of the same level, Chapter 5 Section 5.4), a data field, CRC, and the ACK slot — specialized CAN analyzer software will decode the full frame structure
Reading a capture: compare what you SEE against what you EXPECT, field by field

The core debugging technique for every protocol in this course is the same: take the frame structure from that protocol's chapter (the bit diagrams in Sections 2.1, 3.1–3.2, 4.1–4.3, or 5.3) and check the capture against it, piece by piece. Is the idle level correct? Is the bit timing consistent with the configured rate? Are ACK/NACK bits where you expect? The moment the capture diverges from the expected structure tells you exactly which assumption in your wiring or configuration is wrong — usually far faster than guessing from code alone.

A logic analyzer's own sample rate can lie to you

A logic analyzer samples the digital lines at its own fixed rate — and this is itself a sampling problem, exactly as in Signals & Systems Chapter 6: if the analyzer's sample rate is not comfortably above (ideally 10x or more) the signal's actual bit rate, narrow pulses can be missed entirely or measured with significant timing error, producing a capture that looks subtly wrong even though the actual signal on the wire is perfectly fine. Before trusting a confusing capture, check that the analyzer's sample rate is set high enough for the protocol's actual speed.

Section 6.3

A systematic debugging checklist

This section is a single, protocol-spanning checklist, organized by symptom — the order you should actually check things in, because it goes from fastest/most-common to slowest/least-common.

Symptom: nothing at all is received, ever

  1. Power and ground. Is the far-end device actually powered, and is there a shared ground connection between the two devices? A missing ground reference will cause bizarre, inconsistent behavior across every protocol in this course.
  2. TX/RX crossed (UART only). The single most common UART mistake (Chapter 2, Section 2.4) — verify TX connects to RX and vice versa, not straight across.
  3. Pull-ups present (I2C only). The single most common I2C mistake (Chapter 4, Section 4.1) — measure idle SDA/SCL voltage with a multimeter; it should sit near the supply voltage.
  4. CS wiring and polarity (SPI only). Is the correct CS line being driven, and is it actually reaching the slave (not accidentally tied permanently high)?
  5. Transceiver present (CAN only). Confirm an actual CAN transceiver chip sits between the microcontroller and the bus (Chapter 5, Section 5.5) — the peripheral alone cannot drive the bus.

Symptom: data is received, but it's garbage/scrambled

  1. Baud rate or clock-mode mismatch. UART: confirm both sides use the exact same baud rate, data bits, parity, and stop bits (Section 2.1). SPI: confirm CPOL/CPHA mode matches on both sides (Section 3.2) — this is the classic "looks like a wiring fault but isn't" bug.
  2. Bit order. MSB-first vs. LSB-first mismatch (most relevant to SPI, Section 3.4).
  3. Logic-level mismatch. A 3.3V device trying to read a 5V signal (or vice versa) may read some bits correctly and others incorrectly, producing data that looks almost-but-not-quite right (Chapter 1, Section 1.4).
  4. Clock tolerance exceeded (UART only). If one side uses an inaccurate internal oscillator, check against the formula in Section 2.2 — symptoms often appear only at higher baud rates or with longer frames.

Symptom: works sometimes, fails intermittently

  1. Missing or incorrect termination (RS-485/CAN, long/fast SPI). Check for exactly the right number of termination resistors, in the right places (Chapter 5, Section 5.2).
  2. Buffer overrun (UART, or any protocol with a receive buffer). Is the receiving code reading data often enough, or could a slow main loop be letting the hardware FIFO overflow (Chapter 2, Section 2.3)?
  3. Address collision (I2C only). Two identical devices sharing an address can produce unpredictable, data-dependent corruption rather than a clean total failure (Chapter 4, Section 4.2).
  4. Marginal signal integrity. A too-long single-ended cable, a too-weak pull-up for the bus's actual capacitance, or a clock speed pushed right at a device's rated maximum — all can produce behavior that works most of the time but fails under particular conditions (temperature, specific data patterns, bus load).
Why this checklist is ordered the way it is

Physical-layer problems (power, ground, pull-ups, wiring) are both the most common real-world cause of "it doesn't work at all" and the fastest to check — a multimeter reading takes seconds. Configuration mismatches (baud rate, SPI mode, bit order) are the next most common cause and require comparing documentation or code on both ends. Intermittent failures are, almost by definition, the hardest and slowest to diagnose, because the fault doesn't show up every time you look — which is exactly why checking the first two categories thoroughly before assuming you have a rare, intermittent problem saves enormous amounts of debugging time in practice.

Section 6.4

Worked problem — designing and debugging a multi-sensor system

Problem

You are designing the electronics for a small weather station: a temperature/humidity sensor and a barometric pressure sensor, both mounted together on one small PCB a few centimeters from the microcontroller; an e-paper display mounted on the same PCB, needing to update a full screen's worth of data reasonably quickly; and a separate rain gauge sensor mounted 15 meters away on a pole, connected by a long cable, in an environment with nearby AC power lines. (a) Propose a protocol for each of the three links and justify each choice using this chapter's framework. (b) After wiring it all up, the two sensors on the main PCB are both unresponsive, while the display works fine. List, in the order you'd actually check them, the first three things you'd investigate. (c) The rain gauge connection works perfectly on the test bench (a 1-meter cable) but produces garbled readings once installed outdoors on the actual 15-meter run. What is the most likely cause, and what's the fix?

(a) Temp/humidity + pressure sensors (short distance, multiple devices,
        pins likely worth saving on a small PCB): I2C. Both are short-
        range, low-speed sensors — exactly I2C's sweet spot — and I2C's
        fixed 2-wire cost regardless of device count (Section 6.1's
        table) matters more here than SPI's extra speed, which these
        sensors don't need anyway.

    E-paper display (same board, needs to push a meaningful amount of
        data reasonably quickly): SPI. Updating a full display's worth
        of pixel data benefits from SPI's much higher throughput
        (Chapter 3, Section 3.4) compared to I2C, and display modules
        almost universally offer a dedicated CS pin anyway, so the
        per-device pin cost isn't a real burden for just one display.

    Rain gauge (15 meters away, noisy environment near AC power lines):
        RS-485 (or CAN if the gauge module supports it). Both I2C and
        SPI are ruled out immediately by distance alone — neither is
        rated anywhere near 15 meters (Section 6.1's table) — and the
        nearby AC power lines are exactly the common-mode noise source
        that differential signaling (Chapter 5, Section 5.1) is
        designed to reject.

(b) Checking in the symptom-ordered sequence from Section 6.3:
    1. Measure idle voltage on SDA and SCL with a multimeter — are
       pull-up resistors actually present and pulling the lines to
       near-supply voltage? (The single most common I2C failure, and
       the display working fine on its own separate SPI bus doesn't
       rule this out, since I2C and SPI are electrically independent.)
    2. Run an I2C bus scan (Section 4.3's pattern) to see whether
       EITHER sensor ACKs at any address — this immediately tells you
       whether this is a wiring/pull-up problem (neither responds) or
       an addressing problem (one responds, one doesn't).
    3. If a scan finds nothing at all, re-check SDA/SCL wiring for a
       swap, and check that both sensors are actually receiving power.

(c) The most likely cause is signal reflection and/or noise pickup
    that was negligible over 1 meter but becomes significant over 15
    meters, combined with the described AC power lines as a probable
    noise source — exactly the physical-layer effects Section 5.1 and
    Chapter 1's Section 1.4 describe scaling up with distance. The
    fix, following Section 5.2 directly: confirm proper RS-485
    termination resistors (120 Ω) are present at the two true physical
    ends of the line, confirm bias resistors are defining a clean idle
    state, and confirm a genuinely twisted differential pair (not two
    separate, untwisted wires) is being used for the actual run — an
    untwisted pair loses much of differential signaling's common-mode
    noise rejection, since the two wires no longer pick up near-
    identical noise if they aren't physically coupled together along
    their length.

What to take away: part (a) is this chapter's whole decision framework applied to one realistic design with three genuinely different links, each landing on a different protocol for traceable reasons. Part (c) is a reminder that "works on the bench" is not the same test as "works in the field" — several of this topic's physical-layer effects (reflection, noise, cable-length limits) only become apparent at the actual scale and in the actual environment of final deployment, which is exactly why Chapter 5's termination and signaling-quality requirements matter even when a shortened test setup seems to work fine without them.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. There is no universally 'best' protocol — each one's limitation is the direct, traceable cost of its advantage: UART trades speed for simplicity (Ch. 2's clock-tolerance formula), SPI trades pin count for raw speed (Ch. 3's CS-per-device cost), I2C trades top speed for a fixed 2-wire pin budget (Ch. 4's open-drain mechanism), and CAN/RS-485 trade some complexity/cost for long-distance, noise-immune reliability (Ch. 5's differential signaling).
  2. A logic analyzer or oscilloscope shows what is actually on the wires, which is the fastest way to debug by comparing a real capture against each protocol's expected frame structure (the bit diagrams from Chapters 2–5) field by field. The analyzer's own sample rate must itself be well above the signal's bit rate, or the capture can mislead you — Chapter 6 of Signals & Systems' sampling theorem, applied to the debugging tool itself.
  3. Debug in order of speed and likelihood: check power/ground/wiring and protocol-specific physical essentials (TX/RX crossing, I2C pull-ups, SPI CS, CAN transceiver) first; then configuration matches (baud rate, SPI mode, bit order, logic levels); intermittent failures are usually termination, buffer overruns, address collisions, or marginal signal integrity, and are the slowest category to diagnose — which is exactly why checking the first two categories thoroughly comes first.
  4. Choosing a protocol for a real design means matching device count, distance, speed needs, noise environment, and pin budget against each protocol's known trade-offs — and the same multi-device system often legitimately uses more than one protocol for different links, each chosen for what that specific link actually needs.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

A design needs to connect 10 identical sensor modules, each supporting both SPI and I2C, on a small board where GPIO pins are extremely limited. Which protocol should be chosen, and what specific problem must still be solved?

I2C should be chosen, since its pin cost (2 wires total) does not grow with device count, unlike SPI's requirement of a dedicated CS pin per device (which would need 10 extra pins for 10 SPI devices). The specific problem that must still be solved is that 10 IDENTICAL modules likely share the same fixed I2C address, causing the address-collision problem from Chapter 4 — this would need an I2C multiplexer, or modules with a configurable address pin, to actually distinguish between all 10 devices.
Question 2

While debugging a non-working UART link, you connect a logic analyzer and see the line sitting constantly low, never going high at all, even when you believe nothing should be transmitting. What does this suggest, referencing UART's expected idle behavior?

UART's idle state is HIGH (Chapter 2, Section 2.1) — a line stuck constantly low is not consistent with normal UART idle behavior at all. This suggests either the transmitting device is stuck actively driving the line low (a fault), or the analyzer is actually probing the wrong pin or a disconnected/floating line that has settled low for an unrelated reason, rather than the UART line genuinely idling as UART's own protocol defines it.
Question 3

Following this chapter's debugging checklist order, if an I2C sensor is completely unresponsive, what should you check BEFORE assuming the sensor itself is faulty or that there's a bug in your initialization code?

Following the checklist's symptom-ordered sequence, you should first check power, ground, and specifically whether pull-up resistors are present on SDA and SCL (measurable directly with a multimeter as idle voltage near the supply rail) — this is flagged as the single most common I2C failure and the fastest to check, and should be ruled out before investigating code-level initialization issues or suspecting a faulty sensor, both of which take much longer to diagnose.
Question 4

A design places RS-485 termination resistors at every one of 8 devices spread along a bus, reasoning that 'more termination should mean a more reliable signal.' What is actually wrong with this reasoning, per Section 6.1's trade-off framework and Chapter 5?

Termination resistors are meant to absorb signal energy specifically at the two physical ENDS of a linear bus, where reflections actually occur; placing them at every device (8 resistors instead of 2) significantly over-loads the bus electrically. This can prevent any driver on the bus from successfully pulling the differential lines to a valid logic level at all, since the drivers would be fighting against far too many parallel termination resistors — the opposite of the intended reliability improvement.
Question 5

A sensor link works perfectly in a benchtop test with a 30 cm cable, but fails intermittently once deployed with a 10-meter cable in the final product. Using this chapter's framework, what general category of cause should be investigated first, and why wouldn't code-level debugging be likely to find it?

This should be investigated as a physical-layer / signal-integrity issue — specifically things like signal reflection, insufficient noise immunity for the longer run, or marginal timing margins that only become significant at increased cable length (Chapter 1's Section 1.4 and Chapter 5's termination/signaling requirements). Code-level debugging is unlikely to find it because the application logic and protocol configuration are presumably identical between the working bench test and the failing deployed version — the difference is purely in the electrical behavior of the longer cable, which no amount of inspecting source code can reveal.

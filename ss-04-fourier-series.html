The Fourier Series

Convolution describes an LTI system in the time domain, but it is laborious: sliding, multiplying, integrating. There is a second, often far simpler way to see the same system — by asking what it does to each frequency. This chapter builds the bridge for periodic signals: any periodic signal can be written as a sum of sinusoids at multiples of a fundamental frequency, and an LTI system simply rescales and delays each one. You will learn to compute the coefficients, read a spectrum, use Parseval's relation to count power, and see why a square wave through a low-pass filter comes out rounded — then measure harmonics in real firmware.

Section 4.1

Why sinusoids: eigenfunctions and frequency response

Why sinusoids and not some other family of building blocks? Because of what an LTI system does to them. Feed the complex exponential ejωt into an LTI system with impulse response h(t), and use convolution from Chapter 3:

y(t) = ∫ h(τ) · x(t − τ) dτ                 with x(t) = e^{jωt}
     = ∫ h(τ) · e^{jω(t − τ)} dτ
     = e^{jωt} · ∫ h(τ) e^{−jωτ} dτ           (e^{jωt} does not depend on τ)
     = H(jω) · e^{jωt}

where

H(jω) = ∫ h(τ) e^{−jωτ} dτ                    (the frequency response)

The output is the same exponential, multiplied by a single complex number H(jω). The signal keeps its shape and frequency; only its amplitude and phase change. That is the eigenfunction property promised in Chapter 1, and it is the same idea as an eigenvector in Linear Algebra Ch. 6: the system acts on ejωt as a plain scalar multiplication.

What H(jω) tells you

Write H(jω) = |H|·ej∠H. For a real system, a cosine input cos(ωt) comes out as |H(jω)| · cos(ωt + ∠H(jω)): the magnitude is the gain at that frequency, and the angle is the phase shift. For the RC circuit with h(t) = e−tu(t) (RC = 1 s), H(jω) = ∫0∞ e−τe−jωτ dτ = 1/(1 + jω) — exactly the impedance-divider result you derived in Basic Electronics Ch. 4, now obtained from the impulse response.

The whole plan, in one sentence

If we can write an input as a sum of exponentials, linearity (Chapter 2) lets us push each one through the system separately — each just gets multiplied by H(jω) — and add the results. Convolution becomes multiplication, one frequency at a time. The Fourier series does this for periodic inputs; Chapter 5's Fourier transform extends it to everything else.

Section 4.2

The trigonometric Fourier series

Let x(t) be periodic with period T₀. Define the fundamental frequency ω₀ = 2π/T₀ (rad/s), or f₀ = 1/T₀ in Hz. The trigonometric Fourier series writes x(t) as a constant plus a sum of cosines and sines at the harmonics kω₀, k = 1, 2, 3, …:

x(t) = a₀ + Σ [ a_k cos(kω₀t) + b_k sin(kω₀t) ]      (k = 1 to ∞)

a₀  = (1/T₀) ∫ x(t) dt                       (average over one period)
a_k = (2/T₀) ∫ x(t) cos(kω₀t) dt             (integrals over any one period)
b_k = (2/T₀) ∫ x(t) sin(kω₀t) dt
Why only integer multiples of ω₀?

To make a signal that repeats every T₀, every building block must itself repeat every T₀. cos(kω₀t) does exactly that for integer k, because it completes k full cycles in T₀. A frequency like 1.5ω₀ would not return to its starting value after T₀, and adding it would destroy the periodicity.

Where the formulas come from: orthogonality

Over one period the harmonics are orthogonal:

∫ cos(mω₀t) cos(nω₀t) dt = 0  for m ≠ n,   = T₀/2 for m = n ≥ 1
∫ sin(mω₀t) sin(nω₀t) dt = 0  for m ≠ n,   = T₀/2 for m = n ≥ 1
∫ cos(mω₀t) sin(nω₀t) dt = 0  for all m, n

This is precisely the situation in Linear Algebra Ch. 1 where a dot product of two different basis vectors is zero. To find the coefficient of one harmonic, take the "dot product" (integral of the product) of x(t) with that harmonic; every other term integrates to zero and only the matching one survives, leaving (T₀/2)·a_k. Dividing by T₀/2 gives the formula above. The Fourier series is a change of basis: instead of describing x by its values at each instant, you describe it by its components along the orthogonal basis {1, cos kω₀t, sin kω₀t}.

Worked derivation — the square wave

Let x(t) = +1 for 0 < t < T₀/2 and −1 for T₀/2 < t < T₀. The average is zero, so a₀ = 0. The signal is odd, so every a_k = 0 (cosines are even; Chapter 1's even/odd split says an odd signal has no even components). For the sine coefficients:

b_k = (2/T₀) [ ∫ sin(kω₀t) dt  −  ∫ sin(kω₀t) dt ]      (0 to T₀/2, then T₀/2 to T₀)

∫ from 0 to T₀/2 of sin(kω₀t) dt   = (1 − cos kπ) / (kω₀)
∫ from T₀/2 to T₀ of sin(kω₀t) dt  = (cos kπ − 1) / (kω₀)

b_k = (2/T₀) · 2(1 − cos kπ)/(kω₀) = 2(1 − cos kπ)/(kπ)      using T₀ω₀ = 2π

k odd  (cos kπ = −1):  b_k = 4/(kπ)
k even (cos kπ = +1):  b_k = 0

So:

x(t) = (4/π) · [ sin ω₀t  +  (1/3) sin 3ω₀t  +  (1/5) sin 5ω₀t  +  … ]

A signal made of perfectly flat tops and instant jumps is built entirely from smooth sinusoids — infinitely many of them, with amplitudes falling off as 1/k.

Partial sums of the square wave's Fourier series (time in periods). More terms give a closer fit, but a spike of about 9% of the jump stays at every discontinuity — the Gibbs phenomenon (Section 4.4).
Section 4.3

The complex exponential form and the spectrum

The cosine-and-sine form is easy to picture but awkward to compute with. Using Euler's formula, cos θ = (ejθ + e−jθ)/2 and sin θ = (ejθ − e−jθ)/(2j), the same series collapses into a single sum:

x(t) = Σ c_k · e^{jkω₀t}          (k from −∞ to ∞)

c_k = (1/T₀) ∫ x(t) · e^{−jkω₀t} dt          (integral over one period)

This is the complex exponential Fourier series. Each coefficient ck is a complex number that carries both the amplitude and the phase of the harmonic at kω₀. It connects to the trigonometric form by:

c₀ = a₀
c_k = (a_k − j·b_k) / 2      for k > 0
c_{−k} = conj(c_k)             for any real signal x(t)

Amplitude of the k-th harmonic (a cosine A_k cos(kω₀t + θ_k)):  A_k = 2|c_k|,  θ_k = ∠c_k
Negative frequencies are not a mystery — they are bookkeeping

A real cosine needs two exponentials: ejkω₀t and e−jkω₀t. The "negative frequency" term is simply the complex-conjugate partner that makes the sum come out real. Because c−k = conj(ck), the two halves carry the same information, which is why amplitudes come with a factor of 2: a cosine of amplitude A has c±1 = A/2 each.

The spectrum

Plotting |ck| against k (or against frequency kω₀) gives the line spectrum of the signal: a picture of how much of each harmonic it contains. For the square wave, ck = −j·bk/2 = −j·2/(πk) for odd k, and zero for even k and for k = 0, so |ck| = 2/(π|k|) at odd k.

Line spectrum of the ±1 square wave. The fundamental has |c₁| = 2/π ≈ 0.637; the 3rd harmonic is one third as large; all even harmonics vanish.

Reading coefficients by inspection

When a signal is already a sum of sinusoids, you do not need any integral. Take x(t) = 2 + 3cos(2t) + sin(6t):

Frequencies 2 and 6 rad/s share the largest common ω₀ = 2 (so T₀ = π).
  2                → c₀ = 2
  3cos(2t)         → c₁ = c₋₁ = 3/2                    (k = 1, since 2 = 1·ω₀)
  sin(6t) = (e^{j6t} − e^{−j6t})/(2j)
                   → c₃ = 1/(2j) = −j/2,  c₋₃ = +j/2   (k = 3, since 6 = 3·ω₀)
All other c_k = 0.

Symmetry shortcuts

If x(t) is…Then…Example
Even, x(−t) = x(t)ck is real; only cosines (bk = 0)Triangle or rectangular pulse centered at t = 0
Odd, x(−t) = −x(t)ck is purely imaginary; only sines (ak = 0)The ±1 square wave above
Half-wave symmetric, x(t + T₀/2) = −x(t)Only odd harmonicsThe square wave again

Checking symmetry first can save you from computing half the integrals — or tell you immediately that your answer must have no even harmonics.

Why the complex form wins

It needs one formula instead of three, the coefficients carry amplitude and phase together, and the integrals of exponentials are far easier than integrals of products of trig functions. It also extends directly: let the period T₀ grow to infinity and the sum turns into an integral — that is the Fourier transform of Chapter 5.

Section 4.4

Properties, Parseval, and convergence

Parseval's relation — counting power in the spectrum

The average power of a periodic signal (Chapter 1's definition, averaged over one period) equals the sum of the power in each harmonic:

P = (1/T₀) ∫ |x(t)|² dt = Σ |c_k|²          (k from −∞ to ∞)

Check on the square wave: |x|² = 1 always, so P = 1. From the spectrum: Σ|ck|² = 2·Σk odd ≥1 4/(π²k²) = (8/π²) · (1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + …) = (8/π²)(π²/8) = 1. ✓ The power adds up exactly; nothing is lost in going to the frequency domain. Even better, it shows where the power lives: the fundamental alone holds (2/π)²·2 = 8/π² ≈ 81% of the total.

Useful properties

Operation on x(t)Effect on ck
Linearity: a·x + b·ya·ck + b·dk
Time shift: x(t − t₀)ck·e−jkω₀t₀ — magnitudes unchanged, phase changes by −kω₀t₀
Differentiation: dx/dtjkω₀·ck — high harmonics are amplified
Time reversal: x(−t)c−k

The differentiation rule explains something you already know from control: differentiating a noisy signal amplifies noise. Noise is rich in high frequencies, and multiplying each harmonic by kω₀ boosts exactly those. It is the frequency-domain reason behind the derivative-term warning in Control Systems Ch. 6.

How fast do the coefficients fall? Smoothness decides

Signal has…|ck| falls like…Example
Jump discontinuities1/kSquare wave, sawtooth
Continuous, but with corners1/k²Triangle wave
Smoother stillfaster than any power (for infinitely smooth signals)A pure sinusoid has a single line

The sharper the features in time, the wider the spread in frequency — a theme that returns as the time-bandwidth trade-off in Chapter 5. It is also why sharp switching edges (fast PWM, square-wave clocks) radiate electromagnetic noise at many harmonics, and why designers deliberately slow the edges (slew-rate control) to make a design easier to pass EMC tests.

Convergence and the Gibbs phenomenon

For the series to converge to x(t), the signal must satisfy the Dirichlet conditions: over one period it must be absolutely integrable and have only a finite number of maxima, minima and discontinuities. Every practical periodic signal qualifies. At a jump, the series converges to the midpoint of the two sides (0 for the ±1 square wave).

Adding more harmonics does not remove the overshoot

Near a discontinuity the partial sum overshoots by about 9% of the jump size (here, a peak of about 1.18 for a jump from −1 to +1). Adding more terms squeezes the overshoot into a narrower and narrower spike, but its height does not shrink. This is the Gibbs phenomenon. In practice it means: a brick-wall (sharply truncated) filter applied to a signal with sharp edges produces ringing at those edges, which is one reason real filters use gentle roll-offs.

Section 4.5

Periodic inputs through LTI systems

Now combine Sections 4.1 and 4.3. If a periodic input is x(t) = Σ ckejkω₀t, then by linearity and the eigenfunction property, each term is simply multiplied by H(jkω₀):

y(t) = Σ c_k · H(jkω₀) · e^{jkω₀t}

The output is periodic with the same T₀, its Fourier coefficients are ck·H(jkω₀), and no convolution integral is needed. This is the time-domain-versus-frequency-domain payoff: convolution in time is multiplication in frequency.

Example — a square wave through an RC low-pass

Let x(t) be the ±1 square wave with ω₀ = 1 rad/s, and let the system be the RC circuit with RC = 1 s, so H(jω) = 1/(1 + jω). The input's sine terms have amplitudes b1 = 4/π ≈ 1.273 and b3 = 4/(3π) ≈ 0.424. Evaluate H at each harmonic:

k = 1 (ω = 1):  H(j1) = 1/(1 + j)    →  |H| = 1/√2  ≈ 0.707,   ∠H = −arctan(1) = −45°
k = 3 (ω = 3):  H(j3) = 1/(1 + 3j)   →  |H| = 1/√10 ≈ 0.316,   ∠H = −arctan(3) ≈ −71.6°
k = 5 (ω = 5):  H(j5) = 1/(1 + 5j)   →  |H| = 1/√26 ≈ 0.196,   ∠H = −arctan(5) ≈ −78.7°

Output harmonics (amplitude × |H|, phase shifted by ∠H):
  fundamental:  1.273 × 0.707 ≈ 0.900   → 0.900 · sin(t − 45°)
  3rd:          0.424 × 0.316 ≈ 0.134   → 0.134 · sin(3t − 71.6°)
  5th:          0.255 × 0.196 ≈ 0.050   → 0.050 · sin(5t − 78.7°)

What happened to the shape. The fundamental kept 71% of its amplitude, but the 3rd harmonic kept only 32% and the 5th only 20%. In the input the 3rd harmonic was 1/3 of the fundamental; in the output it is 0.134/0.900 ≈ 1/6.7 of the fundamental — a much smaller share. With the high harmonics suppressed, the sharp corners of the square wave round off into the familiar charge-and-discharge curves. The circuit did not "change" the square wave in some mysterious way: it re-weighted each harmonic by its gain and shifted each one by its phase.

This is what a filter is

A low-pass filter has |H| large at low frequency and small at high frequency; a high-pass is the reverse; a band-pass passes a range. Decomposing a signal into harmonics, weighting each by |H(jkω₀)|, and putting them back together is the entire conceptual content of "filtering". The same idea, with the ω-axis plotted logarithmically, is the Bode plot you will meet in Control Systems II.

Section 4.6

Measuring harmonics in firmware

You will rarely integrate a formula in firmware. Instead you have N ADC samples covering one period, and want the harmonic content. Replace the integral for ck by a sum over the samples:

c_k ≈ (1/N) Σ x[n] · e^{−j2πkn/N}        (n from 0 to N−1)

This is the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) coefficient, and Chapter 6 will explain when it is a faithful estimate. A direct C implementation for one harmonic:

#include <math.h>

// x[] holds N samples covering EXACTLY one period (or a whole number of periods)
void harmonic(const float *x, int N, int k, float *re, float *im) {
    float sr = 0.0f, si = 0.0f;
    for (int n = 0; n < N; n++) {
        float ang = 2.0f * (float)M_PI * k * n / N;
        sr += x[n] * cosf(ang);
        si -= x[n] * sinf(ang);
    }
    *re = sr / N;          // c_k = re + j*im
    *im = si / N;
}

// amplitude of the k-th harmonic (for k >= 1):  A_k = 2 * sqrt(re*re + im*im)
A practical use: total harmonic distortion

To quantify how "dirty" a supposedly sinusoidal signal is (a motor current, an inverter output, mains voltage), compute THD = √(Σk≥2 |ck|²) / |c1|: the power in the harmonics relative to the fundamental. By Parseval's relation, this is the same energy accounting as Section 4.4, done on measured data.

Two ways this quietly goes wrong

(1) The window must hold a whole number of periods. If the buffer cuts a cycle in half, the sum sees an artificial jump at the wrap-around, and energy "leaks" into neighbouring harmonics. (2) The sample rate must be high enough. Harmonic k at frequency kf₀ needs a sampling rate above 2kf₀ to be seen correctly; a harmonic above that limit does not vanish — it reappears at the wrong frequency, disguised as a lower one. Chapter 6 (aliasing and the Nyquist theorem) covers exactly why. Cost: N multiply-accumulates per harmonic; for many harmonics Chapter 5 will introduce a far cheaper algorithm, the FFT.

Section 4.7

Worked problem — the pulse train (PWM) spectrum

Problem

A rectangular pulse train has period T₀. Within one period centered on t = 0, x(t) = 1 for |t| < T₁ and 0 for T₁ < |t| < T₀/2. Take a 50% duty cycle, T₁ = T₀/4. (a) Find the complex Fourier coefficients ck. (b) Which harmonics are missing? (c) Verify Parseval's relation. (d) What would change for a 25% duty cycle?

(a) c_k = (1/T₀) ∫ e^{−jkω₀t} dt    (t from −T₁ to T₁)
        = (1/T₀) · [ e^{−jkω₀t} / (−jkω₀) ]  from −T₁ to T₁
        = (1/T₀) · ( e^{jkω₀T₁} − e^{−jkω₀T₁} ) / (jkω₀)
        = (1/T₀) · 2 sin(kω₀T₁) / (kω₀)
        = sin(kω₀T₁) / (kπ)                        using T₀ω₀ = 2π

    For k = 0 the integral gives c₀ = 2T₁/T₀ = the duty cycle = 1/2.
    With T₁ = T₀/4:  ω₀T₁ = π/2, so   c_k = sin(kπ/2) / (kπ)

      c₀ = 1/2,  c₁ = 1/π,  c₂ = 0,  c₃ = −1/(3π),  c₄ = 0,  c₅ = 1/(5π), …

(b) sin(kπ/2) = 0 for every even k, so all even harmonics vanish
    (the signal is even → c_k real; the 50% duty gives the missing evens).

(c) Time domain: x² = x, so P = (1/T₀)∫x² dt = duty cycle = 1/2.
    Frequency domain: Σ|c_k|² = (1/2)² + 2 · Σ_{k odd ≥1} 1/(π²k²)
                     = 1/4 + (2/π²)(π²/8) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2   ✓

(d) For 25% duty, T₁ = T₀/8: c_k = sin(kπ/4)/(kπ). Now c₀ = 1/4,
    and the zeros fall where kπ/4 is a multiple of π, i.e. every 4th
    harmonic (k = 4, 8, …). A narrower pulse means the first zero moves
    to a higher harmonic: the spectrum spreads out.
Even harmonics vanish; odd ones follow the envelope |sin(kπ/2)|/(kπ). The envelope shape is a sinc function — it returns as the Fourier transform of a single pulse in Chapter 5.

What to take away: the same result gave the harmonic content, the missing-harmonic rule, and a power check in one calculation. Note the connection between (d) and Section 4.4: narrowing the pulse in time widened its spectrum. Also note that this is the spectrum of a PWM signal — the duty cycle of a microcontroller's PWM output (Microcontrollers Ch. 3) sets c₀, which is exactly why a low-pass filter on a PWM pin recovers a DC voltage equal to the duty cycle times the supply.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. An LTI system maps ejωt to H(jω)·ejωt, where H(jω) = ∫h(τ)e−jωτdτ is the frequency response. Exponentials are the eigenfunctions of LTI systems, which is why breaking signals into them makes analysis easy.
  2. A periodic signal with period T₀ (ω₀ = 2π/T₀) is a sum of harmonics at kω₀. The trigonometric series uses a₀, ak, bk; the complex series uses ck = (1/T₀)∫x(t)e−jkω₀tdt, with c−k = conj(ck) for real signals and amplitude Ak = 2|ck|.
  3. The coefficient formulas work because the harmonics are orthogonal over one period — the same idea as a dot product of orthogonal basis vectors. The square wave has only odd sine harmonics with bk = 4/(kπ).
  4. Symmetry shortcuts: even → real ck (cosines only); odd → imaginary ck (sines only); half-wave symmetric → only odd harmonics.
  5. Parseval: average power = Σ|ck|². A time shift changes only phases; differentiation multiplies ck by jkω₀ (amplifying high frequencies). Coefficients fall as 1/k for jump discontinuities, 1/k² for corners. The Gibbs phenomenon leaves a ~9% overshoot at jumps no matter how many terms are added.
  6. Through an LTI system, each harmonic is multiplied by H(jkω₀): output coefficients are ck·H(jkω₀). That is filtering — convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency.
  7. In firmware, ck ≈ (1/N)Σx[n]e−j2πkn/N over a window containing whole periods. A partial period leaks energy; a sample rate that is too low aliases high harmonics (Chapter 6).
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why can a periodic signal with period T₀ contain only frequencies that are integer multiples of 2π/T₀?

Every building block must itself repeat every T₀ so the sum stays periodic. ejkω₀t repeats every T₀ exactly when k is an integer (it completes k full cycles). A non-integer multiple would not return to its starting value after T₀, and adding it would break the periodicity.
Question 2

For x(t) = 2 + 3cos(2t) + sin(6t), what are ω₀, the non-zero ck, and the average power?

ω₀ = 2 rad/s (the largest common frequency). c₀ = 2, c±1 = 3/2, c₃ = −j/2, c−3 = +j/2. By Parseval, P = 2² + 2·(3/2)² + 2·(1/2)² = 4 + 4.5 + 0.5 = 9. Time-domain check: 4 (DC) + 9/2 (cosine) + 1/2 (sine) = 9 ✓.
Question 3

A signal is odd and half-wave symmetric. What can you say about its Fourier series before computing anything?

Being odd means all ak = 0 and a₀ = 0 (only sines; ck purely imaginary). Half-wave symmetry means only odd harmonics appear. So the series contains only sin(kω₀t) terms with odd k — exactly the square-wave structure.
Question 4

You add 100 more harmonics to a square wave's partial sum. Does the overshoot at the edges disappear?

No. The overshoot stays at about 9% of the jump height (a peak near 1.18 for a −1 to +1 jump). More terms make the spike narrower and move it closer to the edge, but do not lower it. This is the Gibbs phenomenon.
Question 5

A square wave with ω₀ = 1 rad/s passes through H(jω) = 1/(1 + jω). By what factor and phase is the 3rd harmonic changed?

H(j3) = 1/(1 + 3j), so the gain is 1/√10 ≈ 0.316 and the phase shift is −arctan 3 ≈ −71.6°. The 3rd harmonic's amplitude 4/(3π) ≈ 0.424 becomes ≈ 0.134, and it is delayed in phase by about 71.6°.
Question 6

Why must a firmware harmonic measurement use a window containing a whole number of periods?

The DFT sum implicitly treats the window as one period repeating. If the window ends mid-cycle, the wrap-around creates an artificial discontinuity, and its energy spreads across many harmonics (spectral leakage), corrupting the measurement.

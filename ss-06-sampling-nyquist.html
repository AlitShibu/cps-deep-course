Sampling & the Nyquist Theorem

Every chapter so far has quietly asked: what does it cost to represent a continuous-time signal as a sequence of numbers? This chapter answers it exactly. Using the Fourier transform from Chapter 5, you will see that sampling copies a signal's spectrum infinitely many times, spaced by the sampling frequency — and that this copying is either harmless or catastrophic depending on one number. That number is the Nyquist rate. You will derive it, see aliasing happen both in the spectrum and on an oscilloscope, learn how reconstruction works (and how it doesn't), and connect all of it to the anti-aliasing filter every real ADC needs.

Section 6.1

Sampling as multiplication: the spectrum repeats

Chapter 1 defined sampling as x[n] = x(nTs). To analyze it with the tools of Chapter 5, model sampling as multiplying the continuous-time signal by an impulse train:

x_s(t) = x(t) · p(t)          where   p(t) = Σ δ(t − nT_s)     (n from −∞ to ∞)

xs(t) is zero everywhere except at the sample instants, where it consists of impulses of strength x(nTs) — a continuous-time stand-in for the sequence x[n]. This trick turns "sampling" into "multiplication by a periodic signal", which Chapter 5's tools already handle.

The spectrum of an impulse train is an impulse train

p(t) is periodic with period Ts, so it has a Fourier series. By symmetry, every coefficient works out to ck = 1/Ts. Using Chapter 5's rule that a periodic signal's transform is 2π Σ ckδ(ω − kωs):

P(jω) = ω_s · Σ δ(ω − kω_s)      (k from −∞ to ∞),   ω_s = 2π/T_s = 2π f_s

A perfectly regular train of impulses in time is, itself, a perfectly regular train of impulses in frequency, spaced by the sampling frequency ωs.

Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency

Chapter 5 proved convolution ↔ multiplication one way; the same proof runs in reverse: multiplying two time signals convolves their spectra (with a 1/2π). Apply that to xs(t) = x(t)·p(t):

X_s(jω) = (1/2π) [ X(jω) * P(jω) ]
        = (1/2π) X(jω) * ω_s Σ δ(ω − kω_s)
        = f_s · Σ X(j(ω − kω_s))          (k from −∞ to ∞)

Convolving with a shifted impulse just copies the function to that shift (Chapter 3's shift property). So the spectrum of the sampled signal is the original spectrum X(jω), repeated every ωs, scaled by fs. This single equation is the entire chapter in one line.

Sampling repeats the spectrum every ω_s. Here the copies are spaced far enough apart that they do not overlap.
A sanity check using Section 5.5

Multiplying by an impulse train is the extreme case of the modulation property from Section 5.5, where instead of one carrier frequency you have every harmonic of ωs acting as a carrier simultaneously. Each harmonic shifts a copy of X(jω) to its own location — which is exactly the picture above.

Section 6.2

The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem

The diagram above assumed the copies of X(jω) stay separated. That requires the gap between copies to be at least the width of one copy. If x(t) is bandlimited — X(jω) = 0 for |ω| > W — the copies do not overlap exactly when:

ω_s − W ≥ W    ⟺    ω_s ≥ 2W    ⟺    f_s ≥ 2f_max

This is the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem: a signal with no content above fmax is completely and exactly recoverable from samples taken at any rate above 2fmax. The quantity 2fmax is the Nyquist rate, and fs/2 is the Nyquist frequency for a given sampling rate.

Why "exactly recoverable" is not an exaggeration

When the copies do not overlap, an ideal low-pass filter (Section 5.4) with cutoff at fs/2, applied to xs(t), keeps only the k = 0 copy — which is X(jω) itself, undistorted. No information was lost by sampling; it is all still there, just repeated. This is a genuinely surprising result: an uncountably infinite continuum of values x(t) is fully captured by a countable list of numbers, provided the bandwidth condition holds.

Why practical systems sample well above the bare minimum

The theorem assumes a perfectly bandlimited signal and an ideal (non-causal, brick-wall) reconstruction filter — neither exists. Real anti-aliasing filters (Section 6.4) roll off gradually, so some margin is needed above 2fmax to let the filter attenuate high frequencies enough before they alias. This is the same causality-versus-sharpness trade-off from Section 5.4.

Section 6.3

Aliasing: what goes wrong below Nyquist

If fs < 2fmax, the spacing ωs is smaller than the width of each copy, and adjacent copies of X(jω) overlap and add. The result is aliasing: high frequencies masquerade as lower ones, and the overlap cannot be undone by any filter — the original information in that overlapped region is genuinely destroyed.

When copies are spaced closer than the signal's own width, they overlap. A low-pass filter can no longer separate the original spectrum from its neighbours.

The single-tone case: the formula you actually use

For a sinusoid at frequency f0, sampled at fs < 2f0, the samples are indistinguishable from a sinusoid at the alias frequency:

f_alias = | f_0 − round(f_0 / f_s) · f_s |

In words: subtract the nearest multiple of fs from f0 and take the magnitude — the result always lands between 0 and fs/2.

Worked examples 
f_s = 1000 Hz  (Nyquist frequency = 500 Hz)

f_0 = 300 Hz:  round(300/1000) = 0  → alias = |300 − 0|   = 300 Hz   (below Nyquist: no aliasing)
f_0 = 700 Hz:  round(700/1000) = 1  → alias = |700 − 1000| = 300 Hz   (looks identical to a 300 Hz tone!)
f_0 = 1300 Hz: round(1300/1000)=1  → alias = |1300 − 1000|= 300 Hz   (same alias again)
f_0 = 900 Hz:  round(900/1000) = 1  → alias = |900 − 1000| = 100 Hz

300 Hz, 700 Hz and 1300 Hz all produce exactly the same sequence of samples at 1000 Hz. Once sampled, there is no way to tell which one was actually present — this is the concrete meaning of "information is destroyed".

A fast sinusoid (thin line) sampled too slowly produces samples that also fit a much slower sinusoid (what your eye connects the dots into) — the classic 'wagon-wheel' aliasing picture.
Aliasing happens to noise too, silently

It is tempting to think "my signal of interest is well below fs/2, so I am safe." But any interference, switching noise, or unfiltered high-frequency content in the input — even if you never intended to measure it — aliases down into your band of interest and corrupts it, indistinguishably from real low-frequency signal. This is why anti-aliasing filtering (Section 6.4) is not optional just because your signal of interest is slow.

The wagon-wheel effect and PWM/strobe artifacts

A camera or the human eye samples continuous motion at a frame rate; a wheel's spokes rotating faster than half that rate appear to spin backwards or stand still — the exact aliasing formula applied to rotational frequency instead of Hz. The same mechanism explains why a fast-spinning fan photographed under an LED strobe (itself pulsing, effectively sampling) can look frozen or slowly turning.

Section 6.4

Reconstruction and the anti-aliasing filter

Since aliasing can never be removed after sampling, it must be prevented before sampling: filter the continuous-time signal so it has (almost) no content above fs/2, then sample. This low-pass filter placed just before the ADC is the anti-aliasing filter.

Reconstruction: turning samples back into a continuous signal

Recovering x(t) from xs(t) means passing it through an ideal low-pass filter with cutoff at fs/2, which keeps only the k = 0 copy from Section 6.1. In the time domain (Section 5.4's ideal low-pass pair, h(t) = sin(Wt)/(πt) with W = ωs/2), this is:

x(t) = Σ x[n] · sinc( π(t − nT_s)/T_s )      (sinc(x) = sin(x)/x)

This is exactly convolution (Chapter 3) of the sample train with a sinc — each sample contributes a scaled, centered sinc pulse, and they add up to reconstruct x(t) perfectly, if Nyquist was satisfied. In practice a DAC does not compute an infinite sinc sum; instead it holds each sample constant until the next one (a "staircase", called zero-order hold), which is a rough approximation of the sinc and is then smoothed by an analog low-pass filter — the DAC's own reconstruction filter.

Why both ends need a filter

FilterWhereJob
Anti-aliasing filterBefore the ADC (analog)Remove content above fs/2 so it cannot fold back into the band of interest
Reconstruction filterAfter the DAC (analog)Smooth the staircase output, removing the repeated spectral copies above fs/2 introduced by sampling
A concrete example: a 10-bit ADC on an ESP32 reading a knob and a motor's back-EMF ripple

Say a control loop samples at fs = 1 kHz and the physical quantity of interest (a slowly turning shaft angle) changes at only a few Hz. But the motor's commutation ripple and PWM switching noise sit at several kHz — well above fs/2 = 500 Hz. Without an analog anti-aliasing filter (even a simple one-pole RC with cutoff around 200–300 Hz), that high-frequency ripple folds down into the 0–500 Hz band and shows up as spurious, slow-looking noise on the reading — noise that no amount of digital filtering afterward can distinguish from real signal, because the fold-down already happened in the analog domain.

A digital low-pass filter cannot undo aliasing that already happened

Chapter 3's FIR/IIR filters operate on x[n] — but if aliasing already occurred during sampling, the corrupted low-frequency content is now indistinguishable from genuine low-frequency signal, because it is literally sitting in the same frequency bin. Digital filtering can clean up noise that is genuinely at high frequency within the digital representation (above the signal, below fs/2), but it is powerless against energy that has already aliased down. The anti-aliasing filter must be analog, and it must come before the ADC.

Section 6.5

Anti-aliasing filters and PWM reconstruction in practice

Chapter 3 built an FIR filter as a fixed impulse response; this section adds one more piece of hardware-adjacent knowledge that decides whether the samples reaching that filter mean anything.

// Example: choosing components for an anti-aliasing filter ahead of a
// 1 kHz sampling loop. Nyquist frequency = 500 Hz. Put the analog RC
// cutoff comfortably below that, leaving margin for the filter's
// gradual roll-off (Section 6.2):
//
//   f_c = 1 / (2*pi*R*C)         choose f_c ~ 150-200 Hz
//   e.g. R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 nF  →  f_c = 1/(2π·10e3·100e-9) ≈ 159 Hz
//
// This single RC stage rolls off at 20 dB/decade (Section 5.4), so by
// 500 Hz (about 1.6 decades above f_c) it has attenuated high-frequency
// noise by roughly 30 dB before it ever reaches the ADC.

#define FS_HZ      1000
#define NYQUIST_HZ (FS_HZ / 2)   // 500 Hz — the hard ceiling for content you can trust
Reading a datasheet with this chapter's eyes

An ADC's maximum sample rate, an audio codec's "sample rate" spec, and a sensor's "output data rate" are all fs from this chapter. When a datasheet recommends an external anti-aliasing filter with a specific cutoff, that number is chosen using exactly the roll-off-versus-margin trade-off of Section 6.2 and 6.4 — not an arbitrary safety factor.

A second, related use: PWM and effective sample rate

A PWM output (Microcontrollers Ch. 3) run through an RC low-pass to make a DC-like analog voltage is reconstruction in miniature: the PWM frequency plays the role of fs, and the filter must remove the switching frequency and its harmonics while passing the intended (usually near-DC or slowly varying) signal. If you try to output a fast-changing analog value via PWM, you need the PWM frequency well above twice the fastest rate of change you want to reproduce — the same Nyquist condition, now applied to a DAC built from a digital output pin.

Undersampling — the deliberate exception

Some systems (software-defined radios, some vibration analyzers) deliberately sample below the signal's own maximum frequency, on purpose, because the signal is narrowband: all its content is confined to a range [flo, fhi] far from 0, even though fhi is large. A more general form of the sampling theorem allows fs to be as low as 2·(fhi − flo) in that case, with the alias intentionally placed at a known, useful location. This is an advanced technique built on exactly the spectrum-copying picture of Section 6.1 — it is mentioned here so the word "undersampling" does not look like a contradiction of everything else in this chapter.

Section 6.6

Worked problem — designing an anti-aliasing filter

Problem

A sensor outputs a signal containing a 2 Hz component of interest plus 50 Hz mains interference and 220 Hz switching noise. It is sampled at fs = 100 Hz with no anti-aliasing filter. (a) Find the Nyquist frequency. (b) Find the alias of each unwanted component. (c) Is the 2 Hz signal itself safe? (d) Design a one-pole RC anti-aliasing filter with R = 10 kΩ that gives at least 20 dB attenuation at 50 Hz, and check what it does at 220 Hz.

(a) Nyquist frequency = f_s / 2 = 50 Hz.

(b) 50 Hz component:  round(50/100) = 0 (or 1, exactly on the boundary)
    → alias = |50 − 0·100| = 50 Hz, landing exactly at the Nyquist edge
      (a worst-case, ambiguous situation — see the pitfall below).

    220 Hz component:  round(220/100) = 2
    → alias = |220 − 200| = 20 Hz
      This lands right on top of the frequency band where the genuine
      2 Hz signal's harmonics or nearby slow dynamics might be studied
      — a serious, silent corruption.

(c) The 2 Hz signal itself is far below 50 Hz Nyquist, so it is not
    aliased. But it is now contaminated by the 220 Hz noise, which
    has aliased down to 20 Hz — an entirely different frequency
    that is much closer to and can be confused with real low-frequency
    content.

(d) Want |H(j2π·50)| ≤ 0.1 (−20 dB) for a one-pole filter
    |H(jω)| = 1/√(1+(ωRC)²) ≈ 1/(ωRC) for ω ≫ 1/RC.
    Set 1/(ω·RC) = 0.1 at f = 50 Hz → RC = 1/(0.1 · 2π · 50) ≈ 0.0318 s
    With R = 10 kΩ: C = 0.0318 / 10000 ≈ 3.18 µF → use C = 3.3 µF (standard value)
    → f_c = 1/(2π·10e3·3.3e-6) ≈ 4.8 Hz

    Check at 220 Hz: |H| ≈ f_c/f = 4.8/220 ≈ 0.022 → about −33 dB,
    comfortably attenuated — the 220 Hz noise is now small enough
    before sampling that its aliased image at 20 Hz is negligible.
    Check at 50 Hz: |H| ≈ 4.8/50 ≈ 0.096 → about −20.3 dB, meets spec.

What to take away: without any anti-aliasing filter, a sample rate that comfortably covers the signal of interest (2 Hz ≪ 50 Hz Nyquist) is still not enough, because other content in the real world does not know or care what you intended to measure. The filter had to be designed around the noise frequencies, not just the signal frequency — and its cutoff (4.8 Hz) ended up well below even the signal itself, illustrating why "sample fast" alone does not solve aliasing; filtering before sampling is what actually solves it.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. Model sampling as multiplying x(t) by an impulse train p(t). Using convolution-in-frequency (Ch. 5), the sampled signal's spectrum is Xs(jω) = fs·Σ X(j(ω − kωs)): the original spectrum, repeated every ωs.
  2. The Nyquist–Shannon theorem: if X(jω) = 0 for |ω| > W, the repeated copies do not overlap when fs ≥ 2fmax (the Nyquist rate). Then an ideal low-pass filter at fs/2 recovers x(t) exactly — no information is lost.
  3. Below the Nyquist rate, copies overlap: aliasing. A tone at f₀ sampled at fs < 2f₀ is indistinguishable from one at falias = |f₀ − round(f₀/fs)·fs|, and this cannot be undone after the fact.
  4. Reconstruction is convolution with a sinc: x(t) = Σx[n]·sinc(π(t−nTs)/Ts). Real DACs approximate this with a zero-order hold plus an analog reconstruction filter.
  5. An anti-aliasing filter (analog, before the ADC) removes content above fs/2 so it cannot fold down; a digital filter afterward cannot separate already-aliased noise from genuine low-frequency signal. Real filters roll off gradually, so fs is chosen with margin above the bare Nyquist rate.
  6. PWM-to-analog reconstruction and deliberate undersampling of narrowband signals are the same spectrum-copying idea applied on purpose, not exceptions to it.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

Why does multiplying a signal by an impulse train correspond to repeating its spectrum, rather than, say, scaling or shifting it once?

The impulse train p(t) is itself periodic, so its own transform is a series of impulses spaced by ω_s. Multiplying x(t) by p(t) in time convolves their spectra, and convolving X(jω) with a train of impulses places a full copy of X(jω) at each impulse location — producing infinitely many repeated copies, spaced by ω_s.
Question 2

A signal is bandlimited to 3 kHz. What is the Nyquist rate, and what happens if it is sampled at exactly that rate versus at 5 kHz?

The Nyquist rate is 2 × 3 kHz = 6 kHz. Sampled at exactly 6 kHz, the copies of the spectrum just touch at the edges — theoretically borderline and fragile in practice, since any real filter has a non-zero transition width. Sampled at 5 kHz (below the Nyquist rate), the copies overlap and aliasing occurs; the signal cannot be recovered exactly.
Question 3

A 1.2 kHz tone is sampled at 1 kHz. What frequency will it appear as, and why can that not be corrected afterward?

round(1200/1000) = 1, so f_alias = |1200 − 1000| = 200 Hz. It cannot be corrected afterward because the samples produced by a genuine 200 Hz tone and by this aliased 1.2 kHz tone are mathematically identical sequences — the information distinguishing them was destroyed at the moment of sampling, not lost in some recoverable way.
Question 4

Why must the anti-aliasing filter be an analog filter placed before the ADC, rather than a digital filter applied to the samples?

Aliasing happens during the sampling process itself, when out-of-band energy folds into the sampled spectrum's baseband. Once that folding has occurred, the aliased energy occupies the same frequency bins as genuine low-frequency content and is indistinguishable from it. A digital filter can only act on the already-sampled data, so it cannot separate the two; the unwanted energy must be removed from the continuous-time signal before it is sampled.
Question 5

A one-pole RC anti-aliasing filter has a gentle roll-off rather than a sharp cutoff. Why does this mean the sampling rate should be set with margin above the bare Nyquist rate rather than exactly at it?

A real filter only gradually attenuates frequencies above its cutoff, so content somewhat above the intended cutoff still passes through with non-negligible amplitude. If the sampling rate were set exactly at twice the intended signal bandwidth, that imperfectly-attenuated content just above the cutoff would alias directly into the band of interest. Extra margin gives the filter's roll-off enough distance to attenuate that content adequately before it reaches the Nyquist frequency.

Putting It Together

Six chapters gave you six separate tools: classify a signal, classify a system, convolve, decompose into harmonics, transform, sample. This closing chapter shows they are one connected story, not six independent topics — and then runs the whole story once, start to finish, on a single realistic problem: designing the digital signal chain for a vibration sensor on a piece of rotating machinery. By the end you will have touched every chapter in one worked design, and you'll see exactly where this topic hands off to Control Systems, Microcontrollers, and the DSP work later in the CPS roadmap.

Section 7.1

The whole topic in one picture

Here is the whole topic as one chain of reasoning, each link forged in an earlier chapter:

Ch.1  A signal is a function. Any signal is a sum of shifted impulses.
         │
Ch.2  A system is LTI if it is linear and time-invariant — the one
      class of system this whole topic (and Control Systems) can
      fully characterize.
         │
Ch.3  Because it is LTI, its impulse response h completely describes
      it: y = x * h  (convolution).
         │
Ch.4  If x is periodic, break it into harmonics c_k. Because e^{jωt}
      is an eigenfunction of LTI systems, each harmonic is just
      scaled and phase-shifted by H(jkω₀).
         │
Ch.5  Drop the periodicity requirement: X(jω) = ∫x(t)e^{−jωt}dt.
      Convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency:
      Y(jω) = X(jω)H(jω). Filtering IS frequency-domain weighting.
         │
Ch.6  To compute any of this on a microcontroller, you must sample.
      Sampling repeats X(jω) every ω_s. Do it too slowly and the
      copies overlap — aliasing — destroying information no filter
      can restore after the fact.
One sentence per chapter, memorized

1: signals are functions, built from impulses. 2: LTI is the class you can analyze. 3: convolution is the LTI system's complete behavior. 4: periodic signals are sums of harmonics that an LTI system merely rescales. 5: convolution becomes multiplication in frequency, for any signal. 6: sampling repeats the spectrum, and Nyquist tells you when that is safe.

The domain triangle

Every signal and every LTI system can be described in (at least) three equivalent ways, and moving between them is a choice of convenience, not a change of meaning:

DomainSignalSystemBest for
Timex(t) or x[n]h(t) or h[n] (impulse response)Causality, stability by inspection (Ch. 2–3), real-time computation
Frequency (Fourier)X(jω) or ckH(jω) (frequency response)Filtering, bandwidth, understanding what a system does to different frequencies (Ch. 4–5)
Laplace / z (Control Systems)X(s) or X(z)G(s) or G(z) (transfer function)Stability via poles, feedback design, transient response

H(jω) is G(s) evaluated at s = jω (Section 5.1); the Nyquist frequency fs/2 is where the unit circle's top sits when a continuous system is turned into a discrete one. None of this is three different subjects — it is one subject described from three vantage points, and Chapter 3's convolution table (Section 3.4) already listed the "reading a property off h" rules that make this triangle work.

Section 7.2

The general design process: physical signal to digital answer

Whenever you take a real, continuous physical quantity and turn it into something a microcontroller can compute on — and then, often, back into a physical output — you are running this same five-stage process. Recognizing the stages is the actual skill this topic was building toward.

The signal chain every embedded sensing/control system follows, and where each chapter of this topic fits.
StageQuestion to askChapter(s)
1. Physical signalWhat is its bandwidth? Is it periodic, transient, noisy?Ch. 1
2. Anti-alias filterWhat is the highest frequency worth keeping? What is contaminating it?Ch. 5 (filtering), Ch. 6
3. Sample (ADC)Is fs ≥ 2×(highest frequency that survives the filter), with margin?Ch. 6
4. ProcessDo you need time-domain smoothing (FIR/IIR) or frequency content (FFT)?Ch. 3 (convolution), Ch. 4–5 (spectrum)
5. Interpret / actIs the system that consumes this output itself LTI? Stable?Ch. 2 (feeds into Control Systems)
The most expensive mistake is always at stage 2

A missing or under-designed anti-aliasing filter is the single most common real error in embedded sensing, because its symptom — noisy-looking, seemingly-random low-frequency corruption — looks exactly like "the sensor is noisy" or "the signal is just like that", and no amount of clever digital filtering afterward can fix it (Section 6.4). Every subsequent stage can be redesigned in software; stage 2 cannot be fixed after the fact.

Section 7.3

Capstone — a vibration-monitoring signal chain, start to finish

The brief. A vibration sensor (an accelerometer) is bolted to a motor housing to detect bearing wear. Healthy bearings produce mostly vibration energy tied to the shaft's rotation, around 25 Hz at normal operating speed. A developing bearing fault adds energy at higher, specific frequencies — for this problem, take the fault signature as living near 400 Hz. The sensor itself responds to vibration up to several kHz, and the environment also injects electrical switching noise from a nearby motor drive at 8 kHz. The goal: sample the signal, verify it is unaliased, and check for the fault signature using a spectrum.

Step 1 — characterize the signal (Ch. 1, Ch. 4)

The vibration is periodic-ish at the shaft rate with harmonics, plus a possible fault component near 400 Hz, plus noise at 8 kHz. Nothing above roughly 1 kHz is of genuine interest for this fault (a design decision, made in consultation with the accelerometer's own frequency response — every real sensor is itself an LTI system with a bandwidth, Ch. 2 and Ch. 5).

Step 2 — design the anti-aliasing filter (Ch. 5, Ch. 6)

Decide the highest frequency worth keeping: fmax = 1 kHz (comfortably above the 400 Hz fault signature, to see a couple of its harmonics too). The 8 kHz noise must be attenuated before sampling. Using Section 6.6's method for a one-pole RC filter, choosing a cutoff around 1.2–1.5 kHz gives roughly:

Target: keep 1 kHz mostly intact, attenuate 8 kHz strongly.
Pick f_c ≈ 1.3 kHz (a bit above f_max, since a single RC pole rolls off gently).

At 8 kHz:  |H| ≈ f_c / f = 1300/8000 ≈ 0.163  → about −15.8 dB
  (a single RC stage may not be enough here — a real design would use
   a steeper 2nd- or 3rd-order active filter to get 30-40 dB by 8 kHz
   without over-attenuating the 1 kHz content; the calculation method
   is identical, just with a steeper roll-off model than 20 dB/decade).

Step 3 — choose the sampling rate (Ch. 6)

Nyquist rate for f_max = 1 kHz  →  f_s ≥ 2 kHz.
Add margin for the filter's non-ideal roll-off (Section 6.2): choose f_s = 5 kHz.
Nyquist frequency = 2.5 kHz — well clear of the content we kept, and the
filter has from 1.3 kHz to 2.5 kHz (roughly one octave) to keep rolling
off before the fold-back point.

Step 4 — process the samples: time domain and frequency domain together (Ch. 3, Ch. 4–5)

Two complementary views, exactly as the domain triangle in Section 7.1 predicts:

// (a) A light digital smoothing filter (Ch. 3) to reject any
//     remaining high-frequency jitter before further processing:
#define N 5
float h[N] = {0.2f,0.2f,0.2f,0.2f,0.2f};   // 5-tap moving average
// (impulse response h; causal, FIR, therefore automatically BIBO
// stable — Section 3.4 — with no risk of ringing like an IIR design)

// (b) An FFT (Ch. 5) on a buffer of, say, N_fft = 1024 samples at
//     f_s = 5000 Hz gives a bin spacing of 5000/1024 ≈ 4.88 Hz —
//     fine enough to clearly separate the ~25 Hz shaft rate from
//     the ~400 Hz fault band (about 82 bins apart).
arm_rfft_fast_instance_f32 S;
arm_rfft_fast_init_f32(&S, 1024);
arm_rfft_fast_f32(&S, samples, spectrum, 0);
arm_cmplx_mag_f32(spectrum, magnitude, 512);
// Then: sum magnitude² in the bins around 400 Hz (bins ~78-86) and
// compare against a healthy-bearing baseline recorded earlier —
// this is Parseval's relation (Section 4.4/5.3) used as a diagnostic.

Step 5 — interpret and act (Ch. 2, → Control Systems)

If the energy in the fault band exceeds a threshold relative to baseline, flag a maintenance alert — and if this feeds an automatic shutdown or speed-reduction control loop, that loop is itself an LTI (or intentionally nonlinear) system whose stability must now be analyzed with Control Systems' tools, using this same signal as its input.

Every earlier chapter shows up somewhere in this design

Chapter 1 (is it periodic, what is its bandwidth), Chapter 2 (is the sensor and the filter LTI, is the overall chain stable and causal), Chapter 3 (the smoothing FIR filter and its impulse response), Chapter 4 (why the shaft rate produces harmonics, and why the fault signature is meaningfully separable from them), Chapter 5 (the anti-aliasing filter's H(jω), and the FFT that turns samples into a spectrum), Chapter 6 (the sampling rate decision and the reason the filter had to come first). Nothing here needed a new idea — only the six chapters already learned, applied in sequence.

What "mastery" of this topic actually looks like

Not memorizing every formula — it is being able to look at any signal-processing situation and immediately ask the right sequence of questions: Is this system LTI? What is its impulse response, or its frequency response? Is the input periodic — if so, what are its harmonics? Is it being sampled — if so, is Nyquist satisfied, and where is the anti-aliasing filter? Section 7.3 was one worked answer to that sequence; the sequence itself is the transferable skill.

Wrap-up

Recap

  1. The whole topic is one chain: signals decompose into impulses (Ch. 1) → LTI systems are fully described by their impulse response via convolution (Ch. 2–3) → periodic signals decompose into harmonics that an LTI system just rescales (Ch. 4) → this generalizes to any signal via the Fourier transform, where convolution becomes multiplication (Ch. 5) → to compute any of it digitally, you must sample without violating Nyquist (Ch. 6).
  2. Every signal and system has three equivalent descriptions — time (x, h), frequency (X, H), and Laplace/z (X(s), G(s)) — related by H(jω) = G(s)|s=jω. Choose whichever domain makes the current question easiest.
  3. The general embedded signal chain is: physical signal → anti-aliasing filter → sample → process (time- or frequency-domain) → interpret/act. The anti-aliasing filter is the one stage that cannot be fixed after the fact.
  4. The capstone applied every chapter to one design: characterizing the vibration signal (Ch. 1, 4), designing the anti-alias filter and sample rate (Ch. 5, 6), processing with both an FIR filter (Ch. 3) and an FFT (Ch. 5), and handing the result to a decision or control loop (Ch. 2, → Control Systems).
  5. This topic is the foundation for Control Systems II (Bode plots), DSP coursework (the z-transform and deliberate filter design), communications (modulation and bandwidth), and any real-time embedded or sensor-processing work.
Check yourself

Quick check

Question 1

In the domain triangle, what does H(jω) correspond to in the Laplace domain, and why does that correspondence only hold for stable systems?

H(jω) is G(s) evaluated at s = jω. This substitution is only valid when the system's region of convergence includes the imaginary axis, which for a causal system is equivalent to the system being BIBO stable (all poles strictly in the left half-plane, Section 2.5/3.4). For an unstable system, the Fourier transform of h(t) may not even exist in the ordinary sense.
Question 2

In the capstone design, why was the anti-aliasing filter's cutoff set above f_max = 1 kHz rather than exactly at it?

A real one-pole (or few-pole) filter rolls off gradually rather than cutting off sharply (Section 5.4). Setting the cutoff exactly at 1 kHz would already attenuate some of the wanted 1 kHz content while barely starting to attenuate frequencies just above it. Placing the cutoff a bit higher preserves the signal of interest and relies on the filter's roll-off, plus the gap up to the Nyquist frequency, to knock down the unwanted 8 kHz noise.
Question 3

Why was the sampling rate chosen as 5 kHz rather than the bare Nyquist minimum of 2 kHz?

The anti-aliasing filter has a gradual roll-off (Section 6.2), so some energy above the intended f_max still leaks through. Sampling well above the bare minimum pushes the Nyquist frequency (2.5 kHz) further from the content being kept, giving the filter more room to attenuate that leakage before it could fold back into the band of interest.
Question 4

If the moving-average filter used in Step 4 were replaced by a recursive (IIR) filter instead, what new check would be required that the FIR version did not need?

An FIR filter is automatically BIBO stable because it has only finitely many, finite impulse-response values (Section 3.4). An IIR filter has a potentially infinite impulse response, so its stability is not automatic — it must be checked, typically via the absolute-summability condition on h[n], or equivalently via pole locations, before it could be trusted in the signal chain.
Question 5

Name one question from each of this topic's six chapters that you would ask when facing a brand-new signal-processing problem.

Representative answers: Ch.1 — is the signal periodic, and what is its bandwidth? Ch.2 — is the system (sensor, filter) linear and time-invariant, causal, and stable? Ch.3 — what is its impulse response, and is it FIR or IIR? Ch.4 — if periodic, what are its harmonic amplitudes? Ch.5 — what does its frequency response or spectrum look like, and where does a filter need to attenuate? Ch.6 — is the sampling rate high enough given the anti-aliasing filter's real roll-off, and where should that filter's cutoff sit?
